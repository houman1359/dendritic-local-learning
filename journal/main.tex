\begin{abstract}
Dendritic trees compartmentalize synaptic inputs, but which spatial distinctions a learning signal must preserve is unclear. We describe delivered credit as combinations of spatial patterns constrained by morphology, and compare tasks that place different demands on those patterns. Image classification benefits mainly from neuron-specific feedback, contextual conflict requires branch selection, hierarchical distractors favor ancestry at an intermediate bandwidth, and input-spectrum-matched pairwise and quartic tasks develop different spatial credit spectra and different tolerance of fixed credit profiles. Reconstructed arbors provide modeled spatial capacity beyond a common broadcast, while focal shunts act as gains on a baseline-weighted ancestry partition, with spatial selectivity depending strongly on electrotonic state. Measured visual responses show no preferential ancestry alignment, delimiting the biological evidence for a framework that links task structure to useful credit resolution without assuming that finer feedback or greater dendritic depth is always advantageous.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Introduction}

Learning must link a synapse's local activity to its contribution to a circuit outcome. Cable filtering, nonlinear integration and spatially organized inhibition make locations within a dendritic tree functionally distinct \citep{rall1962dendrites,koch1999biophysics,poirazi2003pyramidal,london2005dendritic,spruston2008pyramidal,branco2010single,larkum2013cellular}. These differences expand computation, but when does branching also help distribute useful learning signals?

Credit assignment asks how changing a synapse would affect task error. Backpropagation (BP) answers this by calculating the loss gradient---the sensitivity of task error to each parameter \citep{rumelhart1986learning}; related sensitivity calculations apply to recurrent and steady-state networks \citep{almeida1987asynchronous,pineda1987recurrent}. For a point neuron, each incoming-weight gradient is the product of a locally available sensitivity, called eligibility, and a neuron-specific learning signal. Hebbian plasticity captures the association between local pre- and postsynaptic variables \citep{hebb1949organization}, while three-factor rules combine that eligibility with an outcome-dependent signal \citep{fremaux2016threefactor}. Here, a rule is local when a synapse uses its own eligibility and an available learning signal. This definition does not require that the signal itself be computed locally.

Biologically motivated learning theories differ in how they obtain such signals. Feedback alignment communicates errors through separate pathways \citep{lillicrap2016feedback,nokland2016dfa}; equilibrium, predictive and prospective methods infer target states or errors from dynamics \citep{scellier2017equilibrium,whittington2017predictive,song2024prospective}; eligibility propagation combines temporal traces with neuron-specific signals \citep{bellec2020eprop}. One number shared across neurons carries less task information than separately assigned neuronal signals, although each synapse still has its own eligibility \citep{werfel2005learning,whittington2019theories,lillicrap2020backprop}. Recent causal evidence for distinct instructive signals across neuronal populations motivates asking what additional distinctions are useful within a neuron's arbor \citep{francioni2026vectorized}.

We describe this organization using a \emph{route dictionary}: a set of spatial patterns for delivering learning signals. One pattern broadcasts throughout an arbor; several can distinguish subtrees; a complete set can represent every compartment's error. A pattern specifies the relative signal at each location, and its coefficient sets the amount delivered on a given trial. Their linear combinations give the available fields. We use \emph{basis} for linearly independent patterns and \emph{dictionary} when patterns may be redundant. Morphology constrains the available patterns; the task and teaching mechanism determine which combinations are useful.

The same anatomical address can transmit a learning signal with different gains as conductance changes. Excitatory and inhibitory contacts open nonnegative conductances whose reversal potentials determine current. Inhibition can lower input resistance and attenuate other inputs even when its own current is small \citep{koch1983nonlinear,holt1997shunting,chance2002gain,gidon2012inhibition,lovettbarron2012regulation}. Conductance state also changes how a local voltage change affects somatic output and task error. A change in channel amplitude rescales a delivery profile; a change in cable transport can reshape the profile itself.

Dendritic learning theories already provide mechanisms for somatic prediction, segregated basal and apical teaching, interneuron-mediated errors, burst-dependent plasticity and prospective dynamics \citep{urbanczik2014dendritic,schiess2016somatodendritic,guerguiev2017segregated,sacramento2018dendritic,payeur2021burst,ellenberger2026gle,richards2019dendritic}. Detailed cable-learning models use synaptic sensitivities and local approximations to optimize a reconstructed neuron's input--output function \citep{bicknell2021synaptic}. Our question concerns the resolution and geometry of feedback over a whole arbor: when does a morphology-constrained dictionary preserve the distinctions a task requires, and when is a simpler delivery rule sufficient?

The conductance-tree gradient factorization and LocalCA rule were introduced in our predecessor preprint \citep{safaai2026localcredit}. Here we examine how task structure changes the spatial distinctions that credit must preserve, and connect those distinctions to anatomical routes and conductance-dependent transport.

We test a task-to-credit progression: preserving neuron identity on image tasks, selecting the relevant branch under contextual conflict, grouping related branches at limited bandwidth, and resolving example-dependent credit for interactions across branches. Morphology supplies candidate spatial patterns, while conductance controls how those patterns are expressed. Forward representability provides a necessary starting point for interpreting learning, and measured responses test the reach of the anatomical proposal. The central question is which distinctions a useful learning signal must preserve, rather than whether finer feedback is always better.

\section*{Results}

\subsection*{Exact gradients separate local sensitivity from downstream credit}

A useful synaptic update combines local sensitivity with the effect that the change would have on task error. For a point neuron $y_u=f_u(\sum_iw_ix_i+b_u)$, the chain rule gives $\partial\mathcal L/\partial w_i=x_if'_u\delta_u$, where $x_i$ and $w_i$ are synaptic input and weight, $b_u$ is bias, and $\delta_u=\partial\mathcal L/\partial y_u$ is the neuronal error. The derivative $f'_u$ is evaluated at the current input sum. Thus local eligibility $x_if'_u$ and task-derived credit supply different information (Fig.~\ref{fig:framework}A).

We replace the point neuron by a rooted tree with spatially distinct addresses, with soma \(n=0\), compartment voltage \(V_n\), and nonnegative synaptic conductances \(g_i\). A contact with reversal potential \(E_i^{\mathrm{rev}}\) and nonnegative activity \(x_i\) contributes current \(g_ix_i(E_i^{\mathrm{rev}}-V_n)\). In the directed artificial trees, child outputs drive their parents and a terminal-to-soma pass computes the steady state. Total input resistance \(R_n^{\mathrm{tot}}\) is the reciprocal of the summed leak, excitatory, inhibitory and child-coupling conductances (Methods). Differentiating the current balance yields
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial g_i}
=\underbrace{x_iR_n^{\mathrm{tot}}(E_i^{\mathrm{rev}}-V_n)}_{\mathrm{local\ eligibility}}
\underbrace{\delta^V_{n,u}}_{\partial\mathcal L/\partial V_n}.
\label{eq:factorization}
\end{equation}
Both excitatory (E) and inhibitory (I) contacts obey this factorization (Fig.~\ref{fig:framework}A). Eligibility tells how conductance changes local voltage; compartment credit tells how that voltage changes task loss.

Here voltage error is a loss derivative, not a voltage difference. In a directed tree, each compartment has one path to the soma. Its exact voltage error is
\begin{equation}
\delta^V_{n,u}=\delta^V_{0,u}\widetilde\alpha_n,
\qquad
\widetilde\alpha_n=
\prod_{(i\to k)\in\operatorname{path}(n\to0)}
f_i'(V_i)R_k^{\mathrm{tot}}g_{i\to k}^{\mathrm{den}},
\label{eq:pathgain}
\end{equation}
Here \(f'_i(V_i)\) is the child compartment's output derivative, \(g_{i\to k}^{\mathrm{den}}\) is its coupling conductance to parent \(k\), and \(\delta^V_{0,u}=f'_0(V_0)\delta_u\) is the somatic voltage error. Thus the path multiplier \(\widetilde\alpha_n\) converts one somatic error into a spatial field. The conductance-only component of this product is attenuated by additional conductance on a path's ancestors; branch nonlinearities and forward voltages can change as well (Supplementary Section~S1).

Reciprocal cables instead require a joint adjoint calculation of how local currents affect task loss (Methods). These mathematical sensitivities do not establish a biological error-transmission mechanism.

Our local credit assignment (LocalCA) rule retains the local sensitivity but supplies an approximate learning signal to each compartment. A hat denotes this delivered field:
\begin{align}
\widehat\nabla_{g_i}\mathcal L
=x_iR_n^{\mathrm{tot}}(E_i^{\mathrm{rev}}-V_n)\widehat\delta^V_{n,u},
\label{eq:dendriticlocalrule}
\\
\widehat{\bm\delta}^{V}_u=A_u\bm c_u.
\label{eq:routedfield}
\end{align}
Columns of \(A_u\in\mathbb R^{N_u\times K}\) are fixed spatial delivery profiles over \(N_u\) routed nonsomatic compartments; \(\bm c_u\) contains their example-dependent coefficients. Broadcast, subtree and compartment profiles give increasingly detailed spatial addresses (Fig.~\ref{fig:framework}B). This linear delivery model does not follow automatically from the forward cable equations and does not specify how a circuit estimates the coefficients.


At \(K=1\), the all-ones column \(A_u=\bm1\) broadcasts one coefficient through the arbor; at \(K=N_u\), the identity matrix \(A_u=I\) can reproduce the full nonsomatic field. Nested subtrees provide intermediate dictionaries. Profile count, rank (the number of independent profiles) and coefficient information are distinct resources. Exact path transport combines one somatic scalar with state-dependent local derivatives; it does not require an independent external error for every compartment.

Capture is the fraction of squared learning-signal magnitude that a dictionary can reproduce. For a nonzero field \(\bm\delta\),
\[
\mathcal C(A;\bm\delta)=\frac{\|P_A\bm\delta\|^2}{\|\bm\delta\|^2},
\qquad P_A=AA^\dagger.
\]
Here \(P_A\) gives the best least-squares reconstruction from the profiles; \(A^\dagger\) is the Moore--Penrose pseudoinverse and \(\|\cdot\|\) the Euclidean norm. Thus capture is a fraction of squared field magnitude, not a fraction of correct decisions. The mean of these per-field ratios weights fields equally. In contrast, \(\tr(P_AC)/\tr(C)\), with \(C=\mathbb E[\bm\delta\bm\delta^{\mathsf T}]\), is the fraction of total ensemble energy captured, weighting fields by their energy. Here \(C\) is the uncentered second-moment matrix and \(\tr\) denotes its trace, the sum of diagonal entries. The two capture measures need not agree. Projection chooses the best coefficients in the specified metric; an implemented learning rule need not supply them.

The constructions below share this expansion principle but differ in their coordinates, coefficient sources and state dependence; Supplementary Table~S14 compares them. Dictionary capacity answers what fields can be delivered. Learning experiments determine which of those distinctions improve task performance.

\subsection*{One signal per neuron captures most of the image-task learning benefit}

We compared three levels of feedback: one signal shared across neurons, one signal per neuron broadcast through its own arbor, and the exact spatial field (Fig.~\ref{fig:framework}B,C). The decoder supplied exact somatic errors in all three conditions. The strict scalar \(s_i^{\rm sc}\) used their population mean, with a largest-magnitude fallback when the mean nearly cancelled (Methods). Forward networks were otherwise identical.

The primary comparison used flattened MNIST and 128 directed \([3,3]\) trees, each with three proximal and nine terminal compartments. Shunting and topology-matched raw-additive architectures shared the branching graph, contact masks, nonlinearities, decoder and training protocol, with architecture-specific initialization. The additive control retained signed current summation but removed reversal-potential driving forces and the total-conductance denominator. It therefore could not shunt.

Neuron-specific feedback improved accuracy over the strict scalar by 11.2 percentage points in shunting trees and 7.8 points in raw-additive trees. Exact path transport then changed accuracy by less than 0.2 points in either architecture (15 paired seeds; Fig.~\ref{fig:framework}D,E). Most of the scalar-to-exact improvement therefore came from preserving distinct neuronal coordinates. Fashion-MNIST also favored neuron-specific feedback over the matched-width scalar-fallback control. On flattened CIFAR-10, neuron-specific feedback outperformed the strict scalar, but exact path transport reduced accuracy relative to neuron-specific feedback by 0.86 points (95\% paired interval, 0.55--1.17 points; Fig.~\ref{fig:framework}F). Exact transport remained equivalent to matched backpropagation within the predefined margin (Supplementary Fig.~S4).

The historical matched-width fallback retained neuron identity for some near-somatic parameters, improving training. The main MNIST comparison therefore uses the strict scalar (Methods); local eligibilities remain synapse specific in either case.

At common checkpoints, exact factorized transport reproduced automatic differentiation; gradient-cosine diagnostics locate the approximation in the delivered field (Supplementary Fig.~S45C).

The exact field nevertheless retained path-specific structure. Shunting attenuated its absolute magnitude distally, while increasing the distal fraction unexplained by a common signal to 53\%, compared with 32\% in additive trees (Supplementary Fig.~S45D,E). Resolving that structure brought little additional accuracy on MNIST.

Permuting each neuronal coordinate's recipient arbor also reduced accuracy in separate ownership controls, while preserving values and bandwidth (Supplementary Fig.~S45F,G). These tests concern recipient identity rather than finer within-arbor resolution.

The result survived replacing exact between-neuron feedback with a fixed random soma-level matrix. Under direct feedback alignment, neuron-specific feedback improved over a scalar by 10.8 points in additive trees and 11.0 points in shunting trees, while exact path transport added at most 0.09 points. At the neuron and path rungs, random feedback trailed the exact decoder map by no more than about 0.2 points (15 paired seeds; Supplementary Fig.~S30). The within-arbor ordering therefore did not require an exact between-neuron teaching pathway.

These image benchmarks establish that a spatially structured error field need not translate into a benefit from finer feedback. Supplementary Figs.~S4 and S8 retain further depth and task-generator controls. The next experiments introduce task structure that makes particular spatial distinctions useful. A one-step smoothness bound explains how restricting noisy updates can improve expected progress, but its initialization utility lost to a fixed maximum-budget tree in all 20 seed averages of a 12,800-fit prospective selection test, in both selection arms. The bound and noise screens therefore describe local update quality rather than identify an optimal morphology (Methods; Supplementary Sections~S3--S4).

\subsection*{Branch-selective information prevents updates from inactive distractors}

A learning signal can be harmful if it reaches a branch that received an input but did not contribute to the output. To test branch selection directly, we used one logistic output unit with \(B\in\{2,4,8\}\) parallel branch weight vectors and no internal forward compartments or conductance nonlinearity (Fig.~\ref{fig:branchconflict}A). A context selected the branch that determined the output, while every branch received an image and formed an ungated local eligibility. This separates the information needed to select a branch from any specifically dendritic implementation.

Inputs were average-pooled, standardized Fashion-MNIST images from classes 0 and 6. The selected image determined the binary target. At conflict dose \(\chi=0\), all branches received that image; increasing \(\chi\) independently replaced nonselected inputs with opposite-class images. For example \(i\), let \(b_i^\star\) be the selected branch, \(\bm x_{i,b}\) its input vector and \(y_i\in\{0,1\}\) the target. The branch weights \(\bm w_b\) give the pre-probability score (logit) and output error
\begin{equation}
z_i=\bm w_{b_i^\star}^{\mathsf T}\bm x_{i,b_i^\star},
\qquad \delta_i=\sigma(z_i)-y_i.
\label{eq:branchconflictforward}
\end{equation}
Here \(\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})\) is the logistic function. The exact derivative of every unselected branch is zero, irrespective of its input. Increasing conflict therefore does not create opposing nonzero exact branch gradients. It makes a rule that ignores the selector increasingly sensitive to irrelevant, opposite-class eligibilities.

For \(N\) examples, correctly selected and neuron-shared gradient estimates were
\begin{equation}
\bm d_b^{\rm selected}=N^{-1}\sum_i\delta_i\mathbf{1}[b_i^\star=b]\bm x_{i,b},
\qquad
\bm d_b^{\rm shared}=N^{-1}\sum_i\delta_i B^{-1}\bm x_{i,b}.
\label{eq:branchconflictupdates}
\end{equation}
The indicator \(\mathbf{1}[\cdot]\) equals one when its condition holds and zero otherwise. The factor \(1/B\) matches scale but supplies no branch information. The selector can be delivered in feedback or supplied by a branch-local eligibility gate; the resulting update is identical (Fig.~\ref{fig:branchconflict}B). Consequently, the experiment tests access to branch-selective information, not a requirement for several independently computed external error values.

A simplified calculation asks when the selected branch's evidence is outweighed by conflicting evidence sent to other branches. With centered, symmetric class evidence, the shared-mode prediction crosses zero at \(\chi_c=B/[2(B-1)]\): 1, \(2/3\) and \(4/7\) for two, four and eight branches (Fig.~\ref{fig:branchconflict}C; Methods). More branches therefore predict an earlier loss of compatibility. This average calculation is distinct from the conflict dose at which a trained model reaches chance accuracy.

At zero conflict, selected and shared rules achieved similar accuracy. Their difference increased with conflict, reaching 35--58 percentage points at full conflict across the tested branch counts (20 paired seeds; Fig.~\ref{fig:branchconflict}D). In every seed, the advantage of correct routing increased with conflict. A cyclic reassignment sent every signal to the wrong branch at matched rank and sparsity and failed at high conflict. Analytic backpropagation and an explicitly gated grouped-point model matched correct selection exactly, as required by their algebraic equivalence (Fig.~\ref{fig:branchconflict}E; Supplementary Fig.~S29).

The shared rule reached chance at lower conflict doses as branch count increased, consistent with the predicted shift (Fig.~\ref{fig:branchconflict}F). Five two-branch seeds did not cross in the sampled range. Mean-curve, individual-seed and theoretical crossings are different quantities. Checkpoint diagnostics also showed that gradient alignment changed during learning (Methods; Supplementary Fig.~S29D--F). The causal result is that sending an error to branches that received inputs but did not determine the output can harm learning. A dendritic branch is one possible site for this selection.

\subsection*{Ancestry provides a modest advantage at an intermediate feedback budget}

Branch selection compares fully shared with fully resolved feedback. We next asked whether nested subtree routes offer a useful intermediate dictionary. The task contained eight independent, eight-dimensional terminal weight blocks at the leaves of a balanced binary feedback tree. Only the context-selected block determined the logit. Internal tree nodes defined feedback supports and did not perform serial forward computation (Fig.~\ref{fig:subtreefactorial}A).

The selected stream carried the class signal. Distractors carried opposing signals that strengthened with distance through the feedback hierarchy, deliberately favoring task-aligned grouping. At each budget \(K\in\{1,2,4,8\}\), the routing field had \(K\) independent patterns and unit-length rows. Ancestry routes grouped the terminals into the whole tree, halves, sibling pairs or individual terminals. Permuting task streams across leaves changed alignment without changing the streams or forward parameter budget. Here bandwidth counts independent feedback patterns, not physical stages or independent scalar losses; other single-pattern controls need not be uniform. The complete generator and update are given in Methods.

The generator predicts where an intermediate ancestry benefit can occur. The selected stream has class coefficient one; its sibling, same-half nonsiblings and other-half streams have coefficients $-0.15$, $-0.45$ and $-0.75$. The unnormalized sum of the class signal within the selected ancestry group gives $-3.05$, $-0.05$, $0.85$ and $1$ at $K=1,2,4,8$, respectively. Thus coarse pooling reverses or nearly cancels the signal, sibling-pair routing first preserves a positive intermediate-group signal, and full resolution removes the grouping distinction. The implemented unit-row normalization divides these sums by the square root of group size, preserving their signs. This is an analytic consequence of the fixed task design; its location is not an independently discovered optimum (Fig.~\ref{fig:subtreefactorial}B).

Controls preserved rank while changing recipient assignment, support geometry or sparsity: cyclic derangement, depth-interleaving, random-sparse and dense random fields. The per-seed best-control summary takes their maximum after training; it is not a learned selector. A training-informed principal-component reference is retained separately (Methods).

Increasing bandwidth improved correct routing, while derangement caused large losses (Fig.~\ref{fig:subtreefactorial}C). Degree- and depth-preserving rewiring removed the task-aligned advantage at intermediate budgets but did not affect fully shared or fully resolved ancestry routes (Fig.~\ref{fig:subtreefactorial}D). 

The ancestry-specific effect was much smaller. Ancestry performed worse than the best matched control at low budgets, exceeded it by 1.27 percentage points at \(K=4\), and tied at full resolution. The paired \(K=4\) interval was 0.59--1.95 points, with 15/20 positive differences; a paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test gave Holm-adjusted \(P=0.0101\) across four budgets. Individual control contrasts and paired seed differences distinguish this effect from the much larger correct-versus-deranged contrast (Fig.~\ref{fig:subtreefactorial}E,F).

Flat, grouped-point and gated-point representations receiving the same field behaved identically by construction. The experiment therefore supports a conditional advantage of ancestry-shaped feedback under limited bandwidth, without attributing the effect to dendritic material or treating equivalent representations as independent confirmations.

Learning the route coefficients was another limitation. In twenty fresh seeds, an estimator trained on separate noisy context cues improved accuracy over a frozen profile by 7.16 points, but remained 54.47 points below oracle context coefficients. Exploratory selection of its most probable route narrowed the gap, whereas delaying cues removed the benefit (Supplementary Fig.~S32; Methods). These tests expose requirements for selective, timely delivery; they do not derive the context signal from local eligibility.

\subsection*{Cross-branch interactions change the spatial credit that learning uses}

Context and ancestry determine which branches should receive a signal. Interactions between branches can also change the signal's sign and magnitude from example to example. We tested this distinction on tasks with identical input-sensitivity spectra, after ensuring that the same tree could represent both targets. This separates a limitation of credit delivery from a limitation of the forward computation.

Eight independent inputs $x_i$ took values $-1$ or $+1$ with equal probability. Each branch combined child outputs as $u=a+bu_L+cu_R+du_Lu_R$, with four trainable coefficients. Such a unit is multi-affine: it is affine in either child while the other is fixed. Pairwise targets summed four disjoint products, $f_M=\frac12\sum_{(i,j)\in M}s_{ij}x_ix_j$; quartic targets summed products over two complementary quartets, $f_A=\frac12(s_A\prod_{i\in A}x_i+s_{\bar A}\prod_{i\notin A}x_i)$. Signs $s$ were independently sampled. Within each seed, both targets shared a compatible balanced tree, input assignment, initial weights and example streams (Fig.~\ref{fig:prospective}A). Both have input-sensitivity second moment $\E[\nabla_xf\nabla_xf^{\mathsf T}]=I_8/4$: the strengths of independent input-sensitivity directions are identical.

Compatibility has an exact criterion in this model. At each subtree boundary, arrange the target's interaction coefficients in a matrix indexed by products of inputs inside and outside that subtree. After removing the constant inside row, this matrix must have rank at most one, because only one scalar leaves the branch. This condition is also sufficient when it holds at every boundary; discarded singular values bound representation error (Methods; Supplementary Section~S4). It is a prerequisite for the credit comparison, not a theorem about the learning rule. Boolean examples and the complete structure census are retained in Supplementary Figs.~S36--S37 and S43--S44.

We compared exact path credit with unit broadcast and an initially calibrated spatial profile. The calibrated rule averaged each student's path derivatives over 256 unlabeled initialization examples and kept that six-site profile fixed during learning. It could therefore preserve initial signs and relative gains without access to the target. All rules used exact local eligibilities and the same scalar output error. Three development seeds selected learning rates with equal tuning budgets; twenty fresh paired seeds then tested the frozen choices. A separate nested-interaction target used its own compatible tree.

After 1,024 Adam steps, exact and calibrated credit achieved mean held-out normalized mean squared error (NMSE) of 0.0317 and 0.0236 on pairwise targets, compared with 0.1516 and 0.9813 on quartic targets (Fig.~\ref{fig:prospective}B,C). NMSE divides squared prediction error by the clean target variance. On nested targets, exact and calibrated NMSE were 0.0230 and 0.7690 (Fig.~\ref{fig:prospective}D). The predefined task-by-credit contrast was the calibrated-minus-exact gap for quartic targets minus the same gap for pairwise targets: 0.838 NMSE (95\% paired seed-bootstrap interval, 0.719--0.931), positive in all twenty seeds. A common learning rate gave 0.843 (0.727--0.935; Fig.~\ref{fig:prospective}E). These are fixed-budget comparisons; some exact quartic fits retained appreciable error. Equal additive label noise produced different NMSE floors because pairwise and quartic targets have variances one and one-half, respectively.

The learned credit fields explain a distinction that the input spectrum misses. Let $\bm q(x)$ contain the six nonsomatic derivatives of root output with respect to branch output. Exact branch credit is scalar output error times $\bm q$. We first measured how much of the path field's ensemble energy the best single fixed spatial profile could reproduce, allowing an oracle amplitude for each example. At exact-trained checkpoints, that profile captured 99.7\% of pairwise path-field energy, but only 46.1\% for quartic and 44.8\% for nested targets (Fig.~\ref{fig:prospective}F). The separation persisted after loss-error and eligibility weighting and in site-normalized spectra; a replay of 600 earlier fits replicated it (Supplementary Section~S4). Uniform-profile capture did not separate the families: a nearly one-dimensional field can have a signed, nonuniform direction.

Thus identical input spectra can accompany different learned credit spectra on the same tree, and fixed-profile learning fails where the trained field varies in more spatial directions. At identical weights and inputs, however, path derivatives are target-independent. The spectrum is a property of the learner's state and coordinates, not a task-only initialization diagnostic. These observations support a role for example-dependent spatial credit without establishing that all six independent channels, or exact gradients, are necessary.

The effect has a useful boundary. On a compatible positive-conductance teacher, every tested fixed-profile rule learned accurately with Adam; exact credit added no practical accuracy benefit. Boolean controls likewise distinguished cases: broadcast learned aligned XOR-of-AND, whereas parity showed a large exact-credit advantage under the tested optimizer (Supplementary Figs.~S42--S44). Interaction structure therefore identifies comparisons worth testing, rather than a universal monotonic law of credit resolution. Estimating interactions from noisy examples also yielded useful trees; the estimator and its learning pipeline remain supporting structure-design results in Supplementary Figs.~S41--S42.

\subsection*{Serial forward computation and feedback interact with the optimization regime}

The interaction comparison holds a compatible forward tree fixed while changing credit. We next varied serial forward structure itself, asking whether successive compartments can remove unwanted multiplicative changes in input strength (Supplementary Fig.~S31). Physical depth \(D_{\rm p}\) counts forward stages; feedback resolution counts distinctions in the backward field. Neither is interchangeable with the number of latent factors in the data.

Each network contained 64 somata and eight nonsomatic compartments per soma. D1 \([8]\), D2 \([2,3]\), D3 \([2,1,2]\) and D4 \([1,1,2,2]\) redistributed those compartments across one to four serial stages while preserving contacts and trainable resources. Branching vectors are ordered from soma to tips. A literal grouped-point control sent each of the eight modules directly to the soma, removing serial composition. A raw-additive tree retained the directed graph while removing divisive conductance normalization. A dense multilayer perceptron (MLP) was approximately parameter matched and served as a flexible comparison, not as a contact-matched morphology.

Distal excitatory inputs carried class information multiplied by nuisance gains. Separate inhibitory blocks sensed those gains at alignment $\alpha$: zero preserved the sensor distribution but removed its trial-specific information, and one reported the factor exactly. With inhibitory reversal zero, these forward sensor inputs entered the denominator of the conductance equation and could support successive divisions \citep{holt1997shunting,carandini2012normalization}. The generator floored noisy input rates before multiplying them by gains; full equations, floors and sensor controls are specified in Methods.

Three task families separated hierarchical support from generic factor cancellation. Nested factors occupied four, two and one fine-to-global groups. Flat factors used three independent fields on the same eight-group partition, each with its own sensor. Their products were commutative; serial order came from placement of sensors in the model. In the local-ratio task, excitation and inhibition share each module's cumulative gain, permitting local cancellation (Supplementary Section~S5).

Under backpropagation, aligned D3 models exceeded aligned D1 models by 30.9 percentage points. Independent or trial-shuffled sensors and reversed tier placement removed the depth benefit. Raw-additive trees worsened with depth, and the resource-identical parallel grouped-point model did not reproduce the serial computation. More flexible point networks exceeded the serial model. This is therefore a task-matched computational bias under constrained architecture, not a general expressivity advantage of dendrites.

The feedback result depended strongly on optimization. Exact transport exceeded shared-soma transport at D3 by 10.96 points within the LocalCA regime, but full BP exceeded soma broadcast by only 0.64 points under the standard BP optimizer. Optimizer-matched controls explain why the same gradient comparison produced different endpoints (Methods; Supplementary Figs.~S28 and S31). The eleven-point effect belongs to a particular optimization regime; exact-path LocalCA computes the exact selected gradients.

Depth benefits also saturated. The two-factor task favored D2, whereas the four-factor task peaked at D3 rather than D4. Serial models benefited from strongly aligned sensors in both nested and flat factor tasks, more strongly in the nested case. When ratios were locally available, BP reached ceiling in both serial and grouped models, and serial LocalCA could perform worse. Thus forward composition, sensor placement and optimization help determine the utility of a feedback dictionary.

\subsection*{Reconstructed arbors provide spatial routes beyond a shared broadcast}

The artificial tasks show what particular credit patterns can accomplish. We next asked whether reconstructed dendritic geometry supplies useful candidate patterns. MICrONS combines electron-microscopy anatomy and connectivity with co-registered visual responses for a subset of cells \citep{microns2025}. Skeletons, soma positions and mapped contact locations and areas were observed; conductances, voltages and gradient fields were modeled. These analyses therefore test the capacity of measured anatomy to support spatial credit, rather than measure a teaching signal.

Each inhibitory-bearing segment supplied a candidate route over its input-bearing descendants (Fig.~\ref{fig:topology}A). For a site-by-route matrix $A_K$, diagonal excitatory-area weights $W$ and weighted least-squares projector $P_{A_K,W}$, capture of a field $\bm t$ was
\begin{equation}
\mathcal C(A_K;\bm t)=\frac{\|P_{A_K,W}\bm t\|_W^2}{\|\bm t\|_W^2},
\qquad \|\bm v\|_W^2=\bm v^{\mathsf T}W\bm v.
\label{eq:reconstructioncapture}
\end{equation}
For an ensemble of perturbations, numerator and denominator sum over field columns. A common-constrained SVD dictionary provided a ceiling at the same column budget. Controls used random anatomical routes, cable-distance bins, site-shuffled routes preserving nonzero counts, and surrogate trees preserving degree and depth while changing ancestry.

We first examined fields generated independently of the selected dictionary: modeled gradient changes caused by small focal shunts throughout a reciprocal cable. In the original eight-cell analysis, subtree routes lacked a constant component, whereas depth bins could reproduce an all-site broadcast. That difference mattered. A single broadcast captured 31.4\% of total field energy, close to the 32.6\% rank-one ceiling, but most field energy remained outside it. The original comparison therefore mixed the value of a common signal with that of spatial distinctions.

We corrected this resource mismatch by giving every family one budgeted broadcast column and $K-1$ spatial profiles. We measured both total capture and capture of the field remaining after the common component was removed. The protocol was fixed before extending the analysis to 47 disjoint cells from the same mouse and an independently reconstructed second mouse. Each comparison retained actual matrix rank and nonzero coefficient count, because overlapping routes can spend the same nominal budget on different effective resources (Fig.~\ref{fig:topology}).

At eight columns in the disjoint 47-cell cohort, broadcast-plus-subtree routes captured 0.619 of total energy, compared with 0.511 for depth bins and 0.436 for the site-shuffled control (Fig.~\ref{fig:topology}B). The paired ancestry-minus-depth advantage was 0.108 (95\% cell-bootstrap interval, 0.077--0.135), positive in 45 of 47 cells. After removing broadcast, residual-energy capture was 0.527 for ancestry and 0.392 for depth bins (Fig.~\ref{fig:topology}C). Degree--depth surrogates captured 0.578 of total energy; the smaller ancestry advantage over this control was 0.041 (0.020--0.062). Thus coarse geometry accounts for much of the capacity, with additional information in the retained ancestry.

The original eight cells showed the same ordering after common-mode correction (Fig.~\ref{fig:topology}D). The Pinky second-mouse analysis also favored ancestry, but only eight of ten qualifying cells had enough input-bearing sites and candidate routes for the eight-column comparison. Those cells had just 9--13 input-bearing sites, and ancestry nearly saturated their residual fields. We retain their lower-budget comparisons and exclusions separately rather than pool them with the larger arbors. The earlier route-generated positive controls remain in Supplementary Figs.~S10, S20 and S27; they demonstrate capacity under imposed alignment and are distinct from this independent-field extension.

Anatomical labeling and cost measures constrain the interpretation. Direct E/I labels covered 4.71\% of inputs in the disjoint cohort; the 73.0\% direct-versus-proxy agreement was measured among jointly labeled contacts in the original cohort. Nonzero site-by-route coefficients quantify a delivery-cost proxy, not feedback synapses or metabolic expenditure (Fig.~\ref{fig:topology}E). Geometry, labeling and compression sensitivities are reported in Methods and Supplementary Figs.~S12, S25 and S33. The principal result is that ancestry provides spatial capacity beyond a shared broadcast, under matched common-mode access, with its size depending on the arbor and comparison dictionary.

\subsection*{Focal shunting sets gains on ancestry-defined credit routes}

Morphology supplies spatial routes; conductance can regulate their amplitudes. We examined this connection by comparing a focal inhibitory shunt with a current injection matching its baseline focal current in reconstructed reciprocal cables. A separately solved somatic current restored baseline somatic voltage after either intervention. The endpoint was the change in the modeled excitatory-conductance gradient.

Let \(G\) be the passive conductance matrix and \(\bm b\) the current-source vector, so that baseline voltages satisfy \(G\bm V=\bm b\). With \(\bm e_k\) the unit vector at compartment \(k\), adding inhibitory conductance \(\kappa_k\) at compartment \(k\) changes both \(G\) and its source: \(G'=G+\kappa_k\bm e_k\bm e_k^{\mathsf T}\), \(\bm b'=\bm b+\kappa_kE_{\rm I}\bm e_k\). The matched injection supplies baseline current \(\kappa_k(E_{\rm I}-V_k)\) without changing \(G\). The adjoint $\bm q$ gives loss sensitivity to current injected at each compartment. For a fixed somatic loss derivative, it changes by
\begin{equation}
\bm q'=\bm q-\frac{\kappa_kq_k}{1+\kappa_k(G^{-1})_{kk}}G^{-1}\bm e_k.
\label{eq:sherman}
\end{equation}
The column \(G^{-1}\bm e_k\) gives the voltage response to unit current at \(k\). On a passive tree with a somatic loss, the resulting adjoint change has an exact ancestry structure: all descendants of the shunt share the attenuation factor \([1+\kappa_k(G^{-1})_{kk}]^{-1}\), while each sister block along the path to the soma shares its own factor (Fig.~\ref{fig:focal}A; SI S4). These disjoint blocks form an ancestry partition. Weighting their indicators by the baseline soma-to-site transfer gives a dictionary on which the shunt acts through diagonal gains, providing a physical instance of a diagonal channel-gain matrix, denoted \(\Gamma_u\). The diagonal form applies in this partition basis; it need not hold for an arbitrary selected set of overlapping routes.

We compared descendants \(D(k)\) with off-route sites \(C(k)\) at similar depth. A separate kernel criterion tests whether the adjoint change is larger below the shunt (Methods).
The analyzed gradient was sensitivity to open excitatory conductance, \(\gamma_i=q_i(E_{\rm E}-V_i)\). The local driving force \(E_{\rm E}-V_i\) can change differently within each block, so the complete synaptic gradient need not share a single block gain. With primes denoting post-intervention values and \(\epsilon_\gamma\) a numerical floor defined in Methods, we measured absolute log change, \(m_i=|\log[(|\gamma_i'|+\epsilon_\gamma)/(|\gamma_i|+\epsilon_\gamma)]|\), and localization \(\Lambda_k=\operatorname{median}_{D(k)}m_i-\operatorname{median}_{C(k)}m_i\). Positive localization means a larger gradient change below the shunt.

In the permissive normalized model, shunts changed descendant gradients more strongly than depth-matched off-route gradients, with increasing localization at higher dose (Fig.~\ref{fig:focal}B,C). Current injection changed descendant gradients roughly threefold less. Its off-route changes were zero by construction under the soma-restoring control; this is not a general claim that current injection cannot change a gradient. Holding the post-shunt voltage but replacing its adjoint with the baseline adjoint removed most localization, supporting a transport contribution beyond the voltage change alone (Fig.~\ref{fig:focal}D).

Physical calibration sharply limited the effect. At axial resistivity \(R_a=150\,\Omega\,\mathrm{cm}\) and specific membrane resistance \(R_m=15{,}000\,\Omega\,\mathrm{cm}^2\), mean shunt-minus-current localization was small and opposite-signed in the initial eight cells and a disjoint 45-cell cohort from the same mouse: \(-0.0019\) and \(+0.0024\), respectively. Lower membrane resistance increased the contrast by more than an order of magnitude (Fig.~\ref{fig:focal}E). Distributed background conductance produced the same qualitative recovery at fixed high membrane resistance (Supplementary Fig.~S11), consistent with a dependence on electrotonic state \citep{chance2002gain,destexhe2003highconductance}.

Adding weak voltage-dependent channel currents produced a dose response close to the passive response at $R_m=1,000\,\Omega\,\mathrm{cm}^2$ with one baseline-leak unit of distributed background conductance (SI S9). This checks the local linearization around solved steady states. The baseline Jacobian included gate derivatives but remained fixed across doses, so the check does not establish robustness to strongly active dendrites or channel kinetics.

Together, the exact partition identity and the electrical calibration connect the morphology-defined dictionary to conductance-dependent route gains \citep{gidon2012inhibition}. Their spatial selectivity depends on cable state. These imposed, state-matched perturbations measure modeled transport, rather than endogenous error production, observed plasticity or a learning advantage.

\subsection*{Measured responses test ancestry alignment and expose a transfer-geometry limit}

Measured responses ask whether functional relationships preferentially follow ancestry. Seven MICrONS targets had complete arbors and imaged partners: 69 partners in the selected-scan dataset and thirteen eligible scans in the scan-complete analysis. These are recordings during stimulus presentation, not learning.

The direct structure--function test asked whether partners with more shared dendritic ancestry had more similar condition-mean responses, after controlling Euclidean separation and soma-to-contact path-length differences. Mean partial rank correlations were $-0.069$ for selected scans and $-0.048$ for all eligible scans; both target-bootstrap intervals included zero (Fig.~\ref{fig:boundary}A). This empirical test provides no support for preferential ancestry alignment in the sampled partners. Its scale differs from population-level like-to-like wiring \citep{ding2025wiring}.

A separate offline learning comparison used presynaptic responses to predict each target's response through its complete passive tree. Trainable nonnegative excitatory conductances received input proxies at the partners' dominant mapped contact segments; a linear somatic readout predicted the target. Stimulus identities were held out, and transformations and model choices used training data only. Exact learning supplied trial-specific spatial sensitivities. Each restricted rule instead projected the baseline soma-to-site transfer profile $\bm v_0$ into a four-route dictionary $D$, giving $\bm v_D=P_D\bm v_0$, and delivered
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\bm\delta}^{V}_{i,D}=\delta_i^{\rm out}\bm v_D.
\label{eq:micronsrestrictedcredit}
\end{equation}
Here $\delta_i^{\rm out}$ is the scalar output error on trial $i$. The readout and bias always received exact gradients; only input-conductance credit differed.

The realized dictionaries explain what this comparison can test. Selected subtree routes were predominantly distal supports: across scans, they reached 48.0\% of mapped partner inputs and contained an average of 1.31 inputs per route. Several inputs can share one physical segment. Six of thirteen scans had only single-input routes (Fig.~\ref{fig:boundary}B,C). Moreover, one unrestricted fixed baseline-transfer profile already reconstructed 98.4\% of the exact field and 97.6\% of exact updates. The model therefore offered little trial-dependent spatial variation for a richer encoder to exploit. Its learning comparison chiefly diagnoses loss of a nearly fixed transfer profile when that profile is restricted to incompletely covering supports.

Consistent with that geometry, the subtree rule had no reliable held-out advantage over random anatomical routes or site shuffles. Exact learning performed best, while ridge regression captured most of its predictability (Fig.~\ref{fig:boundary}D). Exact learning improved normalized MSE over ridge by 0.015 on average (95\% target-bootstrap interval, 0.004--0.027). Here NMSE is relative to predicting the training mean, unlike the known population-variance normalization in the artificial tasks. Ten split/initialization replicates were averaged within scans and then within targets; the seven targets were the inferential units.

Allowing optimal trialwise coefficients within the same dictionaries barely improved ancestry update reconstruction (Fig.~\ref{fig:boundary}E). For trial-by-parameter update matrices $U$, we measured
\begin{equation}
\mathcal M_\Delta(D)=1-\frac{\|U_{\rm exact}-U_D\|_F^2}{\|U_{\rm exact}\|_F^2},
\label{eq:micronsupdatematch}
\end{equation}
where the squared Frobenius norm sums squared entries. Ancestry rose from 0.232 for its fixed profile to 0.244 with oracle coefficients, compared with 0.385 for random routes and 0.468 for shuffles. Thus the primary limitation was the selected span's geometry, not coefficient freezing. The re-executed checkpoint discrepancies and exact oracle definitions remain in Methods and Supplementary Table~S9.

Observed-input removal and noise controls quantify how response predictability depends on available partners (Supplementary Fig.~S34). Separate imposed-alignment fields establish that the anatomical dictionaries can support learning when the target lies in their span (Supplementary Fig.~S5). Neither supplies a positive control for endogenous teaching in the visual-response recordings. The measured ancestry-similarity test and the modeled transfer diagnostic together delimit what the present data can establish about biological use of the proposed routes.

\section*{Discussion}

Task structure changes which spatial distinctions a useful dendritic learning signal must preserve. The route-dictionary framework makes this question explicit: morphology supplies patterns of delivery, a teaching mechanism supplies their coefficients, and conductance controls their gains. The experiments form a progression from neuronal identity to branch selection, ancestry grouping and example-dependent credit across interacting branches. Useful resolution depends on the task and learner, beyond anatomical detail or exact-gradient fidelity alone.

Image tasks locate a substantial first step at the neuron boundary. Supplying distinct neuronal coordinates accounts for most of the performance gain, including when a random map provides between-neuron feedback. Contextual conflict then creates a need to distinguish active from inactive branches, a distinction that can be supplied either in feedback or by gating local eligibility. Hierarchical distractors create a smaller advantage for ancestry at limited bandwidth. Its four-route location follows from the generator's coefficients, making the experiment a test of a designed prediction about grouping rather than evidence for a universal optimum. These comparisons separate the information delivered from the physical substrate that carries it.

The new interaction comparison gives the strongest link between task structure and spatial credit. Pairwise and quartic targets with identical input-sensitivity spectra share an exactly compatible tree, yet produce different learned credit spectra and sharply different tolerance of fixed spatial profiles. Initial gain calibration and a common learning rate do not remove this difference. A nearly one-dimensional pairwise field can still have poor uniform-broadcast capture, so the relevant issue is the available spatial direction as well as its dimension. The measured spectra describe learned states; they cannot retrospectively validate the failed initialization selector. Nor does failure of the tested profiles establish that every compartment needs an independent external error. Intermediate dictionaries, adaptive profiles and alternative dynamics remain candidate solutions.

Forward structure sets the conditions under which a credit comparison is informative. The cut criterion identifies representationally compatible trees in the scalar multi-affine model, connecting input grouping to hierarchical tensor descriptions \citep{hackbusch2009tensor,grasedyck2010hierarchical,cohen2016tensor}. It also permits constructive structure estimation from noisy examples, retained here as supporting evidence. Conductance trees have their own grouping constraints, and a compatible positive-conductance teacher is learned well with simple credit. Serial composition likewise helps when distributed nuisance factors require successive cancellation, but its interaction with credit depends on optimization and training budget. These results explain why task rank, physical depth and feedback resolution should not be treated as interchangeable quantities.

Reconstructed arbors extend the dictionary proposal to measured geometry. Giving all controls the same common broadcast reveals ancestry capacity in the residual modeled field, including the disjoint 47-cell cohort. The smaller contrast against degree--depth surrogates identifies the contribution beyond coarse geometry. Focal shunting supplies a complementary link to biophysics: in a passive tree its adjoint effect reweights the shunt site's ancestry partition, while synaptic driving forces contribute additional local changes. The electrotonic boundary specifies when those changes remain spatially selective. This account concerns transport of sensitivity and differs from inhibition that computes errors or forward gain--load regulation \citep{rossbroich2023disinhibitory,galloni2026cellular,greedy2026celltype,safaai2026gainload}.

The measured-response analyses delimit the biological claim. Ancestry--response similarity provides an empirical structure--function test and shows no preferential alignment in this small cohort. The offline learning comparison principally tests whether a restricted dictionary preserves an almost fixed cable-transfer profile; its sparse selected supports make it a weak test of endogenous route use. A direct biological experiment would compare plasticity below a focal inhibitory site with electrotonically matched off-route sites while matching somatic voltage, first-order current and the outcome signal. Varying conductance state would test the predicted separation between shared and ancestry-selective effects \citep{francioni2026vectorized,cichon2015branch,bloss2016structured}.

The framework complements somatic prediction, segregated teaching, interneuron-mediated errors and burst plasticity \citep{urbanczik2014dendritic,guerguiev2017segregated,sacramento2018dendritic,payeur2021burst}, as well as cable-learning and engineered gating models \citep{bicknell2021synaptic,chavlis2025dendrites,sezener2021gated,iyer2022activedendrites,lv2025dendritic}. Spikes, temporal eligibility, recurrence and full channel kinetics may change both the available profiles and how their coefficients are estimated \citep{bittner2017btsp,magee2020plasticity}. It separates which distinctions the task demands, which routes the arbor offers and which learning can use.

\subsection*{Conductance models and delivery coordinates}

In a directed tree, $[b_1,\ldots,b_D]$ lists branching factors from soma to tips, giving $\sum_{d=1}^D\prod_{r=1}^db_r$ nonsomatic compartments. A child $c$ supplies output $a_c=f_c(V_c)$. With leak $g_n^{\rm L}$, aggregate open excitatory/inhibitory conductances $G_n^X=\sum_{i\in\mathcal S_n^X}g_i^Xx_i^X$ for contact class $X$, and child couplings $g_{c\to n}^{\rm den}$, the steady voltage is
\begin{equation}
V_n=R_n^{\rm tot}\left(g_n^{\rm L}E_{\rm L}+G_n^{\rm E}E_{\rm E}+G_n^{\rm I}E_{\rm I}+\sum_cg_{c\to n}^{\rm den}a_c\right),
\quad (R_n^{\rm tot})^{-1}=g_n^{\rm L}+G_n^{\rm E}+G_n^{\rm I}+\sum_cg_{c\to n}^{\rm den}.
\label{eq:directedvoltage}
\end{equation}
Here $x_i\geq0$ is contact activity, $g_i\geq0$ its conductance, and $E_X$ its reversal potential. A terminal-to-soma pass evaluates the directed steady state. Artificial conductance trees use dimensionless leak one and reversals $E_{\rm L}=E_{\rm I}=0$, $E_{\rm E}=1$. Monotone parameter transforms enforce nonnegative conductances. Branch-output nonlinearities, termed reactivations in the implementation, are identity functions when disabled. Both excitatory and inhibitory contacts learn in the regular-tree experiments.

For a reciprocal cable residual $\bm F(\bm V,\bm g,\bm x)=0$, the nonsingular voltage Jacobian $J_V$ gives the adjoint
\begin{equation}
J_V^{\mathsf T}\bm q=\nabla_{\bm V}\mathcal L,
\qquad \partial\mathcal L/\partial g_i=-\bm q^{\mathsf T}\partial\bm F/\partial g_i.
\label{eq:generaladjoint}
\end{equation}
With passive current balance, the synaptic gradient is $x_i(E_i^{\rm rev}-V_n)q_n$. This established sensitivity calculation supplies the reference for restricted credit \citep{almeida1987asynchronous,pineda1987recurrent,schiess2016somatodendritic,bicknell2021synaptic}. Directed LocalCA instead combines local eligibility with the supplied voltage-error field as in Eq.~\ref{eq:dendriticlocalrule}; supplying the exact path field recovers the exact selected gradients.

The raw-additive control retains the graph but sums signed inputs without the conductance denominator or reversal-potential driving force. Historical normalized-additive controls additionally standardize voltage across stage coordinates within each example; Supplementary Figs.~S1--S4 identify those variants. The optional four- and five-factor rules add stage-level preconditioners and are described in Supplementary Section~S1.

Writing a delivery dictionary as $A\Gamma$ separates spatial profiles in $A$ from diagonal channel gains in $\Gamma$. Nonzero gains preserve the column span and oracle capture, while changing delivered coefficients and conditioning. A passive focal shunt admits this representation in its baseline-weighted ancestry partition; arbitrary overlapping dictionaries need not. Physical stage count, feedback rank, external error dimension and state-dependent path resolution are distinct resources (Supplementary Tables~S13--S14).

\subsection*{Image feedback ladders and checkpoint diagnostics}

For example $i$, the trained decoder supplies somatic errors $\delta^V_{i,0,u}$ across neurons $u$. The strict scalar is their mean unless its magnitude is at most $10^{-6}$, in which case it is the largest-magnitude coordinate, preserving that coordinate's sign. The same scalar reaches every arbor. Neuron-specific feedback repeats each neuron's own coordinate throughout its arbor; exact transport multiplies it by the local path derivatives. The historical matched-width fallback retained neuronal identity at the 128-wide somatic stage, affecting 640 of 2,413,312 core parameters. A separately trained strict-scalar control tests that exception (Supplementary Table~S16).

The main MNIST ladder uses 128-soma $[3,3]$ shunting and raw-additive networks with fifteen paired seeds per architecture. Within each architecture, contacts, decoder, initialization policy, optimization and validation-based checkpoint selection are shared across credit rules. Fashion-MNIST uses ten paired seeds; the raw-additive CIFAR-10 ladder uses twenty and a 20-soma $[3,3,3,3]$ tree. Images are flattened without convolution or augmentation. The official test sets are held out; the 60,000-image training sets for MNIST and Fashion-MNIST are divided into 48,000 training and 12,000 validation examples. Exact schedules and architecture-specific initialization are retained in Supplementary Section~S5 and resolved configurations.

Direct feedback alignment replaces the decoder derivative by a fixed Gaussian output-to-neuron matrix, scaled by inverse square root of output dimension and seeded by training seed. The factorial retains the same within-arbor rules and pairs new random-feedback fits with the frozen exact-readout reference. Ownership controls permute neuronal recipients without changing coordinate values or bandwidth.

Common-checkpoint diagnostics compare rules at identical weights and states. Exact path transport is checked against automatic differentiation. Gradient cosine, error magnitude by depth and mean-subtracted path energy are distinct summaries; none alone certifies a final learning outcome. The complete image diagnostic sheet is Supplementary Fig.~S45. Historical depth and noise cohorts, including their task-identity and conductance-validity records, are specified in Supplementary Section~S5.

\subsection*{Contextual conflict and ancestry routing}

In the branch-conflict task, one context-selected branch determines a logistic output while all $B$ branches form ungated local eligibilities. Nonselected Fashion-MNIST views are replaced independently by opposite-class images with probability $\chi$. Exact feedback is $\delta_i\mathbf1[b=b_i^\star]$; shared feedback is $\delta_i/B$, where $\delta_i$ is output error and $b_i^\star$ the selected branch. Twenty paired seeds cross $B=2,4,8$ with eight conflict doses. The algebraically equivalent gated-point calculation and BP receive the same updates; cyclic derangement preserves rank and support while changing the recipient.

Under centered, symmetric class evidence, shared-mode compatibility is $s(\chi)=1-2\chi(B-1)/B$, with zero at $\chi_c=B/[2(B-1)]$. This mean-field prediction is separate from a trained accuracy crossing. The latter is reported both by interpolation of the mean curve and by each seed's first sampled at-or-below-chance dose, retaining non-crossings. Checkpoint re-execution reproduces endpoints and compares exact/shared fields at both common and own trained states (Supplementary Fig.~S29).

The ancestry factorial contains eight eight-dimensional blocks. Context $c_i$ and binary label sign $s_i$ are balanced, and inputs are $\bm x_{i,b}=a(b,c_i)s_i\bm t_b+\bm\epsilon_{i,b}$, with seed-specific unit teachers $\bm t_b$ and Gaussian noise $\bm\epsilon_{i,b}\sim\mathcal N(0,1.1^2I_8)$. The selected coefficient is one; sibling, same-half nonsibling and opposite-half coefficients are $-0.15,-0.45,-0.75$. A leaf permutation changes task-to-route alignment without changing the streams. For budget $K$, ancestry feedback has context-by-block entries $\Phi^{(K)}_{cb}=\sqrt{K/8}\,\mathbf1[g_K(\pi(c))=g_K(b)]$, where $g_K$ indexes contiguous groups and $\pi$ maps streams to leaves. Thus every row has unit Euclidean norm.

The update is $\Delta\bm w_b=-(8\eta/N)\sum_i\delta_i\Phi^{(K)}_{c_ib}\bm x_{i,\pi^{-1}(b)}$, with $N$ training examples and rate $\eta$. Each seed supplies 2,048 training, 2,048 test and 1,024 switch examples. Full-batch descent uses rate 0.3, eighty training epochs and twenty switch epochs. Controls include deranged ancestry, depth-interleaved and random-sparse supports, dense rank-$K$ fields and a training-informed PCA reference. The best matched control is the per-seed post-training maximum over the four non-PCA controls. Integer test counts preserve ties in the named Wilcoxon comparison; Holm correction covers four budgets. Alternative mean-sign-flip and historical BH analyses remain explicitly labeled in Supplementary Table~S2. The separate noisy-context encoder and cue-delay experiments are fully specified in Supplementary Section~S5.

\subsection*{Update utility and prospective selection}

Let the exact gradient be $\bm\mu$ and the delivered update estimator have mean $\bm m$ and covariance $\Sigma_{\rm route}$ at the current weights. For an $L$-smooth loss and a nonnegative step $\eta$, expected loss after the update is bounded above by $\mathcal L-\eta\bm\mu^{\mathsf T}\bm m+(L\eta^2/2)(\|\bm m\|^2+\operatorname{tr}\Sigma_{\rm route})$. Optimizing this expression gives utility $U=[\bm\mu^{\mathsf T}\bm m]_+^2/[2L(\|\bm m\|^2+\operatorname{tr}\Sigma_{\rm route})]$, with zero for a zero denominator. Utility certifies one-step progress under the stated moments; it is not a long-horizon ordering.

Synthetic quadratic screens isolate signal retention, noise rejection and fixed gains over sixteen Haar coordinates, without a forward neuron (Supplementary Fig.~S46). Their matched signal/noise hierarchy is a designed crossover. The trained factorial's retrospective utility uses actual full-batch updates and a global logistic-curvature bound; choosing maximum bandwidth matches its selection success. The separate prospective linear experiment retains all 12,800 fits, the initialization selector's loss to fixed maximum-budget and rank-only baselines in every seed, and follow-ups forecasting evolving weights and noise. The 25,600-fit dynamics extension and observed-context-count control are reported in Supplementary Figs.~S35 and S38--S39, with sealed choices, complete methods and computational costs.

\subsection*{Interaction compatibility, fixed profiles and learned spectra}

For a subtree leaf set $S$, $C_S$ arranges the target's Fourier interaction coefficients by products inside and outside $S$; $C_S^\circ$ removes the constant inside row. Scalar multi-affine trees represent the target exactly if and only if every centered cut has rank at most one. If $\mathcal S(T)$ contains all proper cuts of tree $T$ and singular values are ordered from largest to smallest,
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{MSE}(f,\widehat f_T)\geq B(T;f):=\max_{S\in\mathcal S(T)}\sum_{j\geq2}\sigma_j(C_S^\circ)^2.
\label{eq:morphologyselection}
\end{equation}
Overlapping cut tails cannot be added as a lower bound. Supplementary Section~S4 gives the constructive proof, minimum-depth algorithm and its relation to hierarchical tensor representations. The twelve-tree census, noisy-query estimator, end-to-end learning pipeline, conductance grouping controls and Boolean truth tables remain in Supplementary Figs.~S36--S44. The theorem's assumptions do not extend automatically to general conductance ancestors.

The matched credit experiment uses twenty fresh seed blocks after three development seeds select rates with equal tuning budgets. Matching and quartic conditions share tree, leaf assignment, initialization, inputs, additive noise and minibatch order. Exact, unit, initially calibrated and sign-only profiles use the same local eligibilities; calibration averages initial path derivatives over 256 unlabeled examples. Selected and common-rate outcomes are retained for SGD and Adam, giving 432 development and 1,200 fresh fits. Algebraic coefficients remain within $[-2,2]$, and all bound contacts are recorded. Fits stop at 1,024 steps, with seven saved checkpoints. Noisy held-out NMSE divides by clean target variance: one for matching and nested, one-half for quartic. All have noise variance 0.0225. The primary Adam contrast subtracts the pairwise calibrated-minus-exact gap from its quartic counterpart.

For evaluation-example-by-site path matrix $Q$, the best fixed profile is the leading right singular vector. Its capture is $\sigma_1(Q)^2/\sum_j\sigma_j(Q)^2$, an oracle reconstruction with example-dependent amplitudes. Additional diagnostics evaluate uniform, calibrated and ancestry profiles, loss-residual weighting, eligibility-weighted updates and root-mean-square normalization of each site. Participation-ratio effective rank is $(\operatorname{tr}C)^2/\operatorname{tr}(C^2)$ for uncentered $C=Q^{\mathsf T}Q/N$. At fixed weights and inputs, changing labels leaves the path field unchanged; eighty relabeling controls verify this. Independent checkpoint analysis and faithful replay of 600 earlier fits provide numerical and trajectory checks. Complete definitions, pairing audits, optimizer effects and the positive-conductance boundary are in Supplementary Section~S4.

\subsection*{Serial depth, sensor alignment and optimization}

Physical-depth networks contain 64 somata and eight nonsomatic modules each. D1--D4 reallocate these modules across branching vectors $[8]$, $[2,3]$, $[2,1,2]$ and $[1,1,2,2]$. Contacts and resources are matched; serial composition changes. Grouped-point controls connect every module directly to the soma. Raw-additive controls retain the graph without division, while flexible MLP controls are approximately parameter matched.

Excitatory blocks carry class evidence multiplied by lognormal nuisance factors. Inhibitory blocks sense those factors with alignment $\alpha$: zero removes trial-specific information while preserving the sensor distribution, and one reports the factor. The generator floors noisy input rates before multiplying them by gains. Nested, flat and local-ratio families distinguish distributed hierarchical support from generic factor cancellation and locally available ratios. Full generators, floors, sensor-placement controls and contact inventories are defined in Supplementary Section~S5.

Ten paired seeds support each original cohort. The initial maximum is 180 epochs with patience thirty; validation selects model states. BP and LocalCA optimizer recipes differ in learning rates and parameter-group treatment, so their endpoint gaps are reported separately. Exact-path LocalCA computes the exact selected gradients. An audit of one hundred original trajectories verifies every configuration and final metric against its source: all fifty D3 fits across the five relevant credit/optimizer arms reached the epoch cap with validation loss still falling. The original depth contrasts are therefore fixed-budget outcomes. The longer-budget replay protocol and its checkpoint records are retained separately from the original cohort; it restarts original seeds because historical files contain no optimizer state.

\subsection*{Anatomical dictionaries and common-mode controls}

Observed MICrONS skeletons and mapped contacts define soma-rooted branch-to-branch segments. Initial eight and disjoint 47-cell cohorts come from the same mouse; Pinky is a second mouse, with ten of twelve selected cells meeting the inherited typed-contact criterion. Each subtree route contains the input-bearing descendants of its origin. Contact labels are direct where available and otherwise proxy-based as specified in Supplementary Sections~S6--S8. Compression preserves path length but only approximates resistance; direct-label, mapping and geometry sensitivities are retained.

The independent field consists of reciprocal passive-cable gradient changes induced by small focal shunts. Every corrected dictionary receives one constant column and $K-1$ spatial columns. Ancestry selection uses inhibitory fraction times excitatory-area-weighted descendant fraction without field outcomes. Depth bins are reparameterized with the same constant while preserving their span; random routes, independent site shuffles and degree--depth surrogates provide controls. The weighted common component is removed before computing residual-energy capture. A common-constrained SVD oracle supplies the rank-budget ceiling. Numerical rank, coverage and nonzero density are reported separately; equal column count alone does not match those resources.

The original eight cells are post hoc development for common-mode correction. The protocol was frozen before evaluating the new independent-generator outcomes in v661 and Pinky. Two hundred randomized controls are averaged within each cell. At eight columns, all 47 v661 cells and eight Pinky cells qualify; other eligible budgets are retained without substituting for missing eight-column outcomes. Paired cell-bootstrap intervals and Holm-adjusted comparisons describe within-cohort consistency, not animal-population replication. The older route-generated positive controls use separate fields and endpoint definitions (Supplementary Section~S7).

\subsection*{Focal shunting and the electrotonic boundary}

Up to sixteen focal sites per cell span depth quartiles and require at least three excitatory descendants and three comparison sites. Normalized E/I scales are 0.35 with reversals one and $-0.2$. Shunt doses are 0.25, 0.5, one and two times local membrane-plus-synaptic conductance. A matched injection supplies first-order current $\kappa_k(E_{\rm I}-V_k)$ without changing the conductance matrix. A separately solved somatic current restores baseline voltage and is held fixed during differentiation. The squared-loss target is one normalized unit below baseline; finite differences check the adjoint gradients.

Localization subtracts depth-matched off-route from descendant medians of $|\log[(|\gamma_i'|+\epsilon)/(|\gamma_i|+\epsilon)]|$, with $\epsilon=10^{-15}\max(1,\max_i|\gamma_i|)$. Because this is unsigned, signed gradient changes are reported separately. Adjoint and driving-force substitutions in $\gamma_i=q_i(E_{\rm E}-V_i)$ distinguish transport from local voltage effects. Sites and relation-reassignment templates are nested within cells. The passive ancestry-partition identity and its dictionary qualification are derived in Supplementary Section~S4.

Physical calibration uses cylindrical axial conductance $\pi r^2/(R_a\ell)$ and leak $2\pi r\ell/R_m$, expressed in nS, with spherical soma area $4\pi r^2$. Here $r$ and $\ell$ are segment radius and length, $R_a$ axial resistivity and $R_m$ specific membrane resistance. Sweeps include $R_a=100,150,250\,\Omega\,\mathrm{cm}$ and $R_m=300$--$30,000\,\Omega\,\mathrm{cm}^2$, fixed and locally normalized doses, and background conductance. The disjoint calibration cohort contains 235 eligible sites in 45 cells. Geometric fallbacks and dose definitions are specified in Supplementary Section~S9.

The weak-channel check uses $R_a=150$, $R_m=1,000$ in those units and background conductance equal to leak. Median maximal Na, K, Ca and HCN conductances are 0.06, 0.10, 0.025 and 0.05 times leak; NMDA is 0.15 times excitatory conductance. Sixty-four draws per cell passed the predefined steady-state checks. Responses use rank-one updates of the fixed baseline Jacobian, including voltage-gate derivatives, followed by somatic state matching. They are local linearizations rather than newly solved nonlinear equilibria at each dose (Supplementary Fig.~S47).

\subsection*{Measured-response prediction and geometric diagnostics}

The seven-target cohort includes thirteen eligible scans. Repeated responses are averaged by stimulus condition for the ancestry-similarity analysis; partial rank correlations control Euclidean separation and soma-to-contact path-length differences. Offline learning holds out stimulus identities. Training-only percentile transforms produce nonnegative presynaptic proxies, and each partner enters at its dominant mapped contact segment. A linear somatic readout predicts the standardized target response. Only input-conductance credit differs across rules; readout and bias gradients remain exact.

Four-route dictionaries are selected without target responses and receive the projected baseline transfer profile, as in Eq.~\ref{eq:micronsrestrictedcredit}. Realized supports are reconstructed directly from saved segment identifiers. The displayed matrix uses the scan with median mapped-input count, with identifier-based tie breaking; all scan supports are retained. At common exact-trained states, field and eligibility-weighted update reconstruction compare the fixed profile, per-trial field projection and the best within-span update. These are oracle diagnostics rather than newly trained rules. The 130 exact re-executions retain their endpoint discrepancies.

Ridge regularization is selected within training data for every outer split, using the same measured predictors. Observed-input removal, predictor noise and reliability masks are separate sensitivity analyses with no held-out information used for selection. Ten split/initialization replicates are averaged within scans and then within seven targets. Imposed-alignment quadratic fields and the six-animal external signed-credit reanalysis are separate datasets and tests, fully described in Supplementary Sections~S10--S12.
