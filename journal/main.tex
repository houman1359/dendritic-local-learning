\begin{abstract}
Learning in a network is a credit-assignment problem: a synapse should change
only when its change reduces a circuit-level loss. For a point neuron, the
exact gradient separates into a synapse-local eligibility term and a
neuron-specific error supplied by backpropagation. We extend this elementary
decomposition to conductance-based dendritic trees. In a directed tree, each
conductance gradient factorizes into presynaptic activity, input resistance and driving force at
the synapse, multiplied by an error transported from the soma to its host
compartment. Shunting conductance changes both the forward voltage and the gain
of this error transport. The factorization identifies three successive credit
requirements: selecting the correct neuron, selecting locations within its
arbor and scaling each route. A stochastic credit operator then predicts when
a restricted route retains enough task signal to offset representation error,
gain mismatch and noise. Across simulations, neuron-specific feedback provides
the largest benefit; nested-subtree routes help only at intermediate bandwidth,
and physical dendritic depth helps only when matched to task hierarchy.
Reconstructed arbors provide sparse candidate routes, but measured MICrONS
responses do not establish their use for task credit. Dendritic structure is
therefore a conditional routing substrate, not a general solution to credit
assignment.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Introduction}

Consider one computational node \(u\) embedded in a network. Its incoming
weights determine its activity, its activity influences downstream nodes and a
loss \(\mathcal L\) evaluates the network output. Learning asks how each incoming
weight should change to reduce that loss. This is the credit-assignment
problem: the consequence of a local parameter change is defined by a distant,
circuit-level objective. Backpropagation solves the problem exactly by applying
the chain rule from the loss to every parameter
\citep{rumelhart1986learning}. It is therefore the appropriate mathematical
reference even when its literal implementation is not taken as a biological
mechanism \citep{whittington2019theories,lillicrap2020backprop}.

At a point neuron, the chain rule has a simple form. The gradient of an incoming
weight is the product of a locally available sensitivity---presynaptic activity
times the derivative of the postsynaptic nonlinearity---and one error coordinate
specific to the receiving neuron. Classical Hebbian learning emphasized the
local variables at a synapse \citep{hebb1949organization}; supervised and
reinforcement-learning theories later showed that local plasticity must be
combined with an outcome-dependent signal. Modern three-factor rules express
this principle as eligibility multiplied by a modulatory or teaching factor
\citep{fremaux2016threefactor}. The central biological question is therefore
not whether a synapse can form an eligibility trace, but what teaching
information can reach it.

Several families of biologically motivated algorithms approximate this
teaching information in different ways. Feedback alignment replaces exact
transposed weights with alternative feedback pathways
\citep{lillicrap2016feedback,nokland2016dfa}; equilibrium, predictive and
prospective methods infer target states or error variables from network
dynamics \citep{scellier2017equilibrium,whittington2017predictive,
song2024prospective}; and eligibility propagation separates synaptic traces
from neuron-specific learning signals \citep{bellec2020eprop}. These methods
differ mechanistically, but they share a useful abstraction: a synapse combines
a local eligibility with an estimate of the error assigned to its neuron.
Consequently, a scalar broadcast to an entire layer, a neuron-specific signal
and an exact backpropagated error carry different amounts of credit information
\citep{werfel2005learning,lillicrap2016feedback}.

A biological neuron is not a point. Thousands of synapses occupy a branching
dendritic arbor in which cable filtering, nonlinear branches and spatially
organized inhibition shape both forward computation and plasticity
\citep{rall1962dendrites,koch1999biophysics,poirazi2003pyramidal,
london2005dendritic,spruston2008pyramidal,branco2010single,
larkum2013cellular}. In a conductance-based compartment,
excitation and inhibition enter both the current balance and the total
conductance. Increasing inhibitory conductance can therefore reduce local input
resistance and attenuate signals without being equivalent to subtracting a
matched current. This shunting operation is spatially restricted by dendritic
electrotonic structure and depends on operating regime
\citep{holt1997shunting,chance2002gain,gidon2012inhibition,
lovettbarron2012regulation}. A dendritic learning rule must consequently solve a
second credit problem after selecting the correct neuron: it must determine
where within that neuron's arbor the teaching signal should act and with what
gain.

Dendrites have a substantial history in theories of learning. Models have used
somatic prediction, segregated basal and apical compartments, interneuron
circuits, bursts and prospective dynamics to compare or transmit teaching
signals \citep{urbanczik2014dendritic,schiess2016somatodendritic,
guerguiev2017segregated,sacramento2018dendritic,payeur2021burst,
ellenberger2026gle,richards2019dendritic}. Detailed cable sensitivities have also been used to
optimize the input--output function of a reconstructed single neuron
\citep{bicknell2021synaptic}. These studies establish that dendrites can
participate in credit assignment. They do not, however, determine whether a
complete arbor is a useful, bandwidth-limited routing system for an error that
originates at the circuit level. That question requires separating three
operations that are often conflated: choosing a neuron-specific error
coordinate, assigning refinements of that coordinate to locations within the
tree and setting the gain along each route.

Here we develop this separation from first principles. Replacing a point node
by a conductance-based dendritic tree changes one error coordinate into a
soma-transported compartment field while retaining a local eligibility--error
factorization. The forward conductance equation identifies presynaptic
activity, input resistance and driving force as eligibility and shows how
shunting changes transport gain. This turns dendritic credit assignment into a
representation problem: how well can biological routes approximate the exact
compartment-error field?

We answer this question with a stochastic credit operator that maps the exact
gradient to the field available to a local rule. Its utility depends on retained
task signal, representation error, route-gain error and admitted noise. The
experiments then add credit information in the same order as the derivation:
from a scalar signal, to a neuron-specific coordinate, to a within-arbor
address, to exact transport. Subsequent analyses distinguish address depth from
physical depth, test conductance-dependent gain, and ask whether reconstructed
arbors provide and use the predicted routes. The resulting account is
conditional: dendrites can supply sparse routing structure, but that structure
improves learning only when its resolution, gain and topology match the credit
required by the task (Fig.~\ref{fig:framework}).

\section*{Results}

The derivation defines four feedback objects: a layer-wide scalar, a
neuron-specific coordinate, within-arbor addresses and the exact
compartment-resolved error. Exact errors are information ceilings; gains
reproduced by point models with matched parameter groups, routes and gains
reflect structured credit rather than uniquely dendritic material.

\subsection*{From a network node to a local dendritic learning rule}

Consider a point neuron \(u\) with presynaptic activities \(x_i\), incoming
weights \(w_i\), preactivation \(z_u\) and output \(y_u\):
\begin{equation}
z_u=\sum_{i\in\mathcal I_u}w_i x_i+b_u,
\qquad y_u=f_u(z_u).
\label{eq:pointforward}
\end{equation}
The network loss \(\mathcal L\) depends on \(w_i\) only through this node and
the downstream network. Define the neuron-specific error coordinate
\(\delta_u\equiv\partial\mathcal L/\partial y_u\). The chain rule gives
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial w_i}
=\underbrace{x_i f_u'(z_u)}_{e_i:\,\text{local eligibility}}
\underbrace{\delta_u}_{\text{neuron-specific coordinate}} .
\label{eq:pointneuron}
\end{equation}
For a hidden node, backpropagation computes this coordinate recursively from
its downstream neighbors,
\begin{equation}
\delta_u=\sum_{v:\,u\to v}w_{uv}f_v'(z_v)\delta_v,
\label{eq:backproprecursion}
\end{equation}
starting from the derivative of the loss at the output. Equation~\ref{eq:pointneuron}
therefore separates the exact gradient into two conceptually different
objects. The eligibility \(e_i=x_i f_u'(z_u)\) uses variables available at the
synapse and its postsynaptic neuron. The error coordinate \(\delta_u\) contains
the downstream, task-dependent credit that is not locally determined.

A local three-factor rule preserves the eligibility and substitutes an
available teaching signal \(\widehat\delta_u\) for the exact coordinate:
\begin{equation}
\Delta w_i=-\eta e_i\widehat\delta_u.
\label{eq:pointlocalrule}
\end{equation}
It equals gradient descent when \(\widehat\delta_u=\delta_u\); otherwise its
quality depends on how much of the correct neuron-specific error the feedback
path conveys. This eligibility--teaching decomposition links classical local
plasticity and modern three-factor rules to backpropagation without assuming a
biological implementation of Eq.~\ref{eq:backproprecursion}. Here
\emph{local learning} means that the plasticity calculation at synapse \(i\)
uses its own eligibility and a communicated teaching coordinate, rather than a
parameter-specific downstream Jacobian. It does not mean that the teaching
coordinate is independent of the global task.

We next replace point neuron \(u\) by a rooted dendritic tree with soma
\(n=0\). Compartment \(n\) receives synaptic conductances \(g_i\), presynaptic
activities \(x_i\) and reversal potentials \(E_i\); \(\mathcal S_n\) includes
both excitatory and inhibitory contacts. Leak conductance \(g_n^L\) has
reversal \(E_L\), and \(g_{c\to n}^{\mathrm{den}}\) couples child \(c\) to its
parent. Each child transmits \(a_c=f_c(V_c)\), and the somatic output is
\(y_u=f_0(V_0)\). The compartmental dynamics underlying the directed model are
the current balance
\begin{equation}
C_n\frac{dV_n}{dt}
=g_n^L(E_L-V_n)
+\sum_{i\in\mathcal S_n}g_i x_i(E_i-V_n)
+\sum_{c\in\mathcal C_n}g_{c\to n}^{\mathrm{den}}(a_c-V_n).
\label{eq:directedcurrentbalance}
\end{equation}
The simulations evaluate the steady state, obtained by setting the left-hand
side of Eq.~\ref{eq:directedcurrentbalance} to zero. Collecting the terms that
multiply \(V_n\) gives
\begin{align}
g_n^{\mathrm{tot}}
&=g_n^L+\sum_{i\in\mathcal S_n}g_i x_i
  +\sum_{c\in\mathcal C_n}g_{c\to n}^{\mathrm{den}},\\
V_n
&=R_n^{\mathrm{tot}}\left(
g_n^LE_L+\sum_{i\in\mathcal S_n}g_i x_iE_i
+\sum_{c\in\mathcal C_n}g_{c\to n}^{\mathrm{den}}a_c\right),
\qquad R_n^{\mathrm{tot}}=(g_n^{\mathrm{tot}})^{-1}.
\label{eq:directedvoltage}
\end{align}
Thus \(V_n\) is a conductance-weighted steady-state voltage. A contact \(j\) is
inhibitory when its reversal is \(E_j=E_I\). Its effective conductance
\(g_jx_j\) enters \(g_n^{\mathrm{tot}}\) even when \(V_n\simeq E_I\) and its
net current \(g_jx_j(E_I-V_n)\) is small. It can therefore lower local input
resistance and attenuate other inputs without acting like a matched negative
current. This divisive effect is shunting.

The same steady-state equation determines the conductance gradient. Holding
the presynaptic activities and child outputs fixed, quotient differentiation
gives
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial V_n}{\partial g_i}
=\frac{x_iE_i g_n^{\mathrm{tot}}-x_i(g_n^{\mathrm{tot}}V_n)}
       {(g_n^{\mathrm{tot}})^2}
=x_iR_n^{\mathrm{tot}}(E_i-V_n).
\label{eq:localsensitivity}
\end{equation}
Applying the chain rule from the loss to \(V_n\) then gives
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial g_i}
=\underbrace{x_iR_n^{\mathrm{tot}}(E_i-V_n)}_{\text{local eligibility}}
\underbrace{\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial V_n}}_{\text{compartment error}},
\label{eq:factorization}
\end{equation}
where presynaptic activity, input resistance and driving force are local to the
synapse or its host compartment. All remaining task dependence lies in the
compartment error. Equation~\ref{eq:factorization} applies to excitation and
inhibition alike. Writing an inhibitory parameter as \(g_j^I\),
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial g_j^I}
=x_jR_n^{\mathrm{tot}}(E_I-V_n)
\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial V_n}.
\label{eq:shuntinggradient}
\end{equation}
Near the inhibitory reversal, the direct driving-force factor can be small,
while the same conductance still changes \(R_n^{\mathrm{tot}}\) and therefore
the sensitivities of other synapses and compartments. This distinction between
the local gradient of a shunt and its effect on transported credit is central
to the perturbation experiments below.

Let \(p(n)\) be the parent of compartment \(n\), and let
\(\delta_{0,u}=\partial\mathcal L/\partial V_0\) be the exact somatic error of
neuron \(u\), including its downstream network derivative. The chain rule along
one dendritic edge is
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial V_n}
=\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial V_{p(n)}}
 f_n'(V_n)R_{p(n)}^{\mathrm{tot}}g_{n\to p(n)}^{\mathrm{den}}.
\label{eq:compartmentrecursion}
\end{equation}
Because a directed tree has one path from \(n\) to the soma, repeated
application yields
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial V_n}
=\delta_{0,u}\widetilde\alpha_n,\qquad
\widetilde\alpha_n=
\prod_{(i\to k)\in\operatorname{path}(n\to0)}
f_i'(V_i)R_k^{\mathrm{tot}}g_{i\to k}^{\mathrm{den}} .
\label{eq:pathgain}
\end{equation}
The path gain \(\widetilde\alpha_n\) contains every branch derivative, coupling
conductance and downstream input resistance between compartment \(n\) and the
soma. Thus dendrites do not change the basic eligibility--error logic of
Eq.~\ref{eq:pointneuron}; they replace one neuron-specific error coordinate by a
spatial field of compartment errors.

Shunting acts directly on this transport. Consider the partial effect of an
added inhibitory conductance \(G_k^I\) on the conductance-only path gain
\(\alpha_n^{\mathrm{cond}}\), holding the state-dependent branch derivatives
and coupling conductances fixed. Then
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial\log\alpha_n^{\mathrm{cond}}}{\partial G_k^I}
=-R_k^{\mathrm{tot}}\,\mathbf 1\!\left[k\in\operatorname{path}(n\to0)\right].
\label{eq:shuntpathgain}
\end{equation}
The direct gain effect is zero off the path and negative on the path, with a
magnitude set by local input resistance. Equation~\ref{eq:shuntpathgain}
isolates a possible route-gain mechanism; it does not establish a learning
advantage. A physical shunt also changes the forward voltage, driving forces
and possibly branch derivatives, and its net effect can vanish outside a
permissive electrotonic regime. At the regular-tree operating point the
mechanism is visible descriptively: the conductance-stage path-gain field was
far less dispersed under shunting than in the matched additive model
(coefficient of variation 0.229 versus 1.161, an 80.3\% reduction, with the
same ordering in all five paired seeds; Supplementary Fig.~S1A). This is an
operating-point, unweighted statistic; it does not by itself establish an
improved gradient direction or a learning benefit.

The directed path product is a special case of the Almeida--Pineda steady-state
adjoint \citep{almeida1987asynchronous,pineda1987recurrent}. For any
differentiable compartmental system
\(\bm F(\bm V,\bm g,\bm x)=\bm 0\), define
\(J_V=\partial\bm F/\partial\bm V\). Implicit differentiation gives
\begin{equation}
J_V^{\mathsf T}\bm q=\nabla_{\bm V}\mathcal L,
\qquad
\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial g_i}
=-\bm q^{\mathsf T}\frac{\partial\bm F}{\partial g_i}.
\label{eq:generaladjoint}
\end{equation}
For the residual convention \(\bm F=G\bm V-\bm b\), the exact conductance
gradient is
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial g_i}
=\underbrace{x_i(E_i-V_n)}_{\text{local synaptic factor}}
\underbrace{q_n}_{\text{compartment adjoint}}.
\label{eq:generalconductancegradient}
\end{equation}
Here \(q_n\) is the loss sensitivity to a small current injected at compartment
\(n\). In the directed model,
\(q_n=R_n^{\mathrm{tot}}\partial\mathcal L/\partial V_n\):
Eq.~\ref{eq:factorization} displays input resistance in the eligibility,
whereas Eq.~\ref{eq:generalconductancegradient} absorbs it into the adjoint. In a
reciprocal passive cable, the same error is transported by the Green's function
\(G^{-1}\) rather than a single path product \citep{rall1962dendrites}. This
connects the present derivation to recurrent adjoints, cable theory and earlier
detailed-arbor learning based on cable sensitivities
\citep{schiess2016somatodendritic,bicknell2021synaptic}.

The local dendritic rule preserves the exact eligibility but replaces the
compartment error with an available feedback field:
\begin{equation}
\widehat\nabla_{g_i}\mathcal L
=x_iR_n^{\mathrm{tot}}(E_i-V_n)\widehat\delta_{u,n}.
\label{eq:dendriticlocalrule}
\end{equation}
It is exact when
\(\widehat\delta_{u,n}=\partial\mathcal L/\partial V_n\). Restricted feedback
instead represents the field using \(K\) communicated coordinates
\(c_{u,k}\) and a fixed route dictionary \(A_{u,nk}\):
\begin{equation}
\widehat\delta_{u,n}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{u,nk}c_{u,k}.
\label{eq:routedfield}
\end{equation}
A global scalar uses one value for an entire layer. A neuron-specific field
uses one coordinate for each neuron and repeats it across that neuron's
compartments. An addressed field uses multiple coordinates per neuron; a
nested-subtree route sets \(A_{u,nk}=1\) for all descendants of one branch
point and zero elsewhere. Coordinate-to-arbor assignment specifies which tree
receives each neuronal coordinate, while path conductance sets route gain. The
vector of exact compartment errors is the \emph{credit field}; field capture is
the fraction retained by a restricted dictionary.

Equations~\ref{eq:factorization} and \ref{eq:pathgain} matched automatic
differentiation across 100 diagnostic runs (maximum relative error
\(1.88\times10^{-7}\)). We next determine how much is gained by selecting the
correct neuron and then ask whether within-arbor addresses add useful credit.

\subsection*{Neuron-specific feedback resolves the dominant information bottleneck}

Equation~\ref{eq:pointlocalrule} identifies the first requirement of a local
rule: feedback must distinguish neurons that require different updates. Before
asking whether dendrites provide useful within-arbor addresses, we therefore
compared three levels of information (Fig.~\ref{fig:feedback}a). A scalar
broadcast supplied one error
value to an entire layer. Neuron-specific feedback supplied one coordinate
\(\widehat\delta_u\) to each cell and repeated it across that cell's
compartments. Exact transport supplied the complete field
\(\partial\mathcal L/\partial V_n\). The scalar comparison tests a known
feedback-bandwidth limitation \citep{werfel2005learning,lillicrap2016feedback};
the exact condition asks whether substantial error remains after the correct
neuron has been selected.

In regular trees with two branching stages and three children per branch
(\([3,3]\) trees), neuron-specific feedback increased MNIST accuracy from
\(85.88\%\) to \(97.13\%\) in shunting networks (mean paired difference,
11.249 percentage points; 95\% paired-seed interval, 10.446--12.032) and from
\(89.31\%\) to \(97.12\%\) in matched additive networks (7.809 points;
7.237--8.431). All 15 paired seeds improved in both architectures (two-sided
sign-flip \(P=6.10\times10^{-5}\); Fig.~\ref{fig:feedback}b). Exact
compartment-wise transport then changed
shunting accuracy by only \(0.045\) percentage points (95\% paired-seed
interval, \(-0.018\) to \(0.102\); 10 positive, 4 negative and 1 tied seed;
two-sided sign-flip \(P=0.186\)). In the additive network it produced a small
but reliable \(0.186\)-point improvement (\(0.115\) to \(0.257\); 14/15
positive seeds; \(P=3.05\times10^{-4}\)), reaching \(97.31\%\). Thus selecting
the correct neuron accounted for 99.6\% and 97.7\% of the mean
scalar-to-exact accuracy gap in shunting and additive networks, respectively;
perfect within-tree transport was a secondary, architecture-dependent
correction.

The strict control also exposed a high-leverage detail in the historical
implementation. The legacy scalar-fallback arm retained neuron coordinates in
only 640 near-somatic parameters (0.027\% of the core), yet it outperformed
strict scalar by 4.859 points in shunting networks (3.994--5.669; 15/15 seeds)
and 2.274 points in additive networks (1.796--2.699; 14/15, one tie). Thus a
small near-somatic parameter set cannot be dismissed by parameter count alone.
Conversely, strict scalar learning did not make the neurons identical: it
still reached \(85.88\%\) and \(89.31\%\), because the shared teaching value
multiplied neuron- and synapse-specific eligibility traces. The limitation was
the dimensionality of the teaching signal, not a collapse of the hidden layer
to one neuron.

An independent fixed-checkpoint diagnostic, evaluated at networks trained
with neuron-specific feedback, found that replacing the legacy
matched-width/scalar-fallback field with neuron-specific sharing raised mean
local--exact gradient cosine from approximately zero to 0.708 in shunting
trees and 0.625 in additive trees (Fig.~\ref{fig:feedback}d). In a separate
five-seed MNIST shunting
implementation check, the factorization-derived local rule with exact
transport reached \(97.18\%\), compared with \(97.03\%\) for matched
backpropagation (Supplementary Fig.~S3C). Together, these controls show that most of the scalar deficit
arose because the feedback pathway failed to identify the receiving neuron,
not because the synapse-local eligibility in
Eq.~\ref{eq:factorization} was incorrect.
Initialization-matched controls found no general accuracy advantage for the
shunting forward model, so conductance is treated below as a possible
route-gain mechanism rather than a universally better optimizer.

We next varied physical dendritic stage count and task. Here D1--D4 denote one
through four serial dendritic stages in the forward model; they are
architectural depths, not cell types or feedback-route depths. Of 640 planned
runs, 480 satisfied the positive-conductance input-validity criterion; the
excluded signed-rate conditions cannot define a valid shunting denominator and
are reported in Methods. Across the retained runs, neuron-specific feedback
improved accuracy over the legacy matched-width/scalar-fallback field by
4.92--6.99 percentage points on MNIST (Fig.~\ref{fig:feedback}g) and
9.76--14.16 points on the additive noise task; all 120 retained seed pairs
improved (Supplementary Fig.~S19). Exact transport placed mean
local-rule accuracy within 0.14
points of matched backpropagation across all 12 retained task--model--depth
conditions. Fashion-MNIST reproduced the neuron-specific gain relative to the
same fallback field (4.46 points for shunting and 3.78 for additive trees,
10/10 paired seeds each) but found no
reliable additional benefit of exact path transport
(Fig.~\ref{fig:feedback}c,f). Selecting the correct neuron was therefore the
dominant improvement across datasets, architectures and depths; perfecting
transport within that neuron produced a smaller and less consistent gain.

Neuron selection and within-neuron routing are distinct
(Fig.~\ref{fig:feedback}e). To isolate the first,
we preserved every neuron-specific coordinate and the total feedback bandwidth
but permuted which arbor received each coordinate. Correct
coordinate-to-arbor assignment added 0.15--0.70 points and was favored in
58/60 pairs, much smaller than the coordinate-bandwidth gain
(Fig.~\ref{fig:feedback}f). An independent 320-run implementation check found
no material exact-transport--backpropagation difference across depth, task and
forward model. Physical depth itself was nevertheless costly at fixed terminal
branch and contact counts: D4 underperformed D1 in all 40 valid additive
seed--feedback comparisons, by 7.92 points under the matched-width fallback and
1.06--1.37 points with neuron-specific, exact or backpropagated credit
(Supplementary Figs.~S8 and S19). Depth therefore magnifies poor feedback; it
does not generically improve learning.

The remaining question is whether one neuron-specific coordinate is sufficient
when different parts of the same neuron require different updates. We first
constructed a minimal two-stream credit-reversal task in which one somatic
error implies different credit for sibling subtrees. Correct two-subtree
routing reached \(80.63\%\), compared with \(76.24\%\) for one shared neuronal
coordinate and \(19.63\%\) after fixed sibling-route derangement (ten paired
seeds; Fig.~\ref{fig:feedback}h). Exact transport, analytic backpropagation and
a point model with the same context-gated parameter groups matched correct
routing exactly. This controlled task proves that a within-neuron address can
carry useful information, but also that the advantage is not
dendrite-exclusive: an explicitly gated point model implements the same
parameter assignment. The next experiment asks when the tree's own nested
addresses are competitive with matched non-anatomical routes.

\subsection*{Structured subtree addresses help at an intermediate feedback budget}

Once the correct neuron receives an error coordinate, a dendritic route can add
information only by allowing different parts of that neuron to receive
different refinements. In Eq.~\ref{eq:routedfield}, \(K\) is the number of
communicated feedback coordinates per neuron. At \(K=1\), one value is shared
through the tree; at \(K=8\), every terminal branch in the present model can be
addressed independently. Intermediate \(K\) values ask whether the nested
subtrees of the arbor provide a useful low-dimensional basis.

We tested this question with an eight-context hierarchical credit-reversal
task. The experiment evaluated \(K\in\{1,2,4,8\}\), six route families, three
reference conditions and five representation-matched implementations across
20 paired seeds, producing all 2,700 planned fits. The correct route family
used the same nested subtrees as the task hierarchy. Controls preserved
feedback rank, route norm and forward resources but used deranged subtree
assignments, depth bins, random sparse groups, random rank-\(K\) fields or a
learned rank-\(K\) task basis. Dendritic,
explicitly gated point, flat-compartment and grouped-point implementations each
used 64 parameters, 64 active contacts and one nonlinearity. A degree- and
depth-matched rewired tree preserved these resources while breaking the match
between task hierarchy and tree topology.

Increasing feedback rank improved the correctly routed model: mean held-out
accuracy was 0.187, 0.449, 0.801 and 0.810 for \(K=1,2,4,8\), respectively
(Fig.~\ref{fig:subtreefactorial}b). Deranging the within-tree assignment caused
large losses at \(K=2\) (paired difference 0.264, 95\% bootstrap interval
0.243--0.285) and \(K=4\) (0.611, 0.601--0.620). These contrasts show that an
address can matter when the task requires different subtree updates.

The stronger question is whether the tree-defined address is better than any
matched non-anatomical address. It was not generally better. The correct
nested-subtree route lost to the best matched control at \(K=1\) and \(K=2\),
won by 0.0127 at \(K=4\) (0.0059--0.0197; 15/20 seeds; two-sided Wilcoxon
\(P=0.0048\), Benjamini--Hochberg-adjusted \(P=0.0064\)), and tied at full rank
\(K=8\) (Fig.~\ref{fig:subtreefactorial}c). At low bandwidth, learned and
random rank-\(K\) routes represented the low-dimensional task more efficiently.
At full rank, route choice became irrelevant because every leaf had its own
coordinate. The advantage of the nested tree therefore occurred only at the
intermediate budget \(K=4\).

That intermediate advantage depended on task--topology alignment. The
task-matched tree exceeded the degree- and depth-matched rewired tree by 0.231
(0.210--0.254) at \(K=2\) and 0.050 (0.042--0.060) at \(K=4\), with all 20
seeds positive in both contrasts (Fig.~\ref{fig:subtreefactorial}d). Rewiring
had no effect at \(K=1\), where the field was shared, or at \(K=8\), where it
was fully resolved. Exact transport and analytic backpropagation were
identical. The four representation-matched dendritic and point
implementations also matched exactly whenever they received the same routed
fields (maximum accuracy difference 0; Fig.~\ref{fig:subtreefactorial}e).
The result is therefore a benefit of structured parameter addressing, not of
dendritic material alone.

Field capture explained the large bandwidth and assignment failures but not
the complete learning outcome. In the matched tree, trained nested-subtree
routes retained 0.162, 0.315, 0.547 and 1.000 of the weighted exact coefficient
field at \(K=1,2,4,8\); deranged assignments retained zero for \(K>1\). Across
the route ladder, capture and held-out accuracy were positively associated in
every seed (mean within-seed Spearman \(\rho=0.824\), 95\% seed-bootstrap
interval 0.810--0.838; \(\rho=0.639\), 0.610--0.665 after excluding the exact
\(K=8\) boundary). Yet nested subtrees, depth bins and random sparse groups
could have similar capture and different trained outcomes
(Fig.~\ref{fig:subtreefactorial}f). A theory must therefore account not only for
how much of the mean gradient a route represents, but also for alignment,
gain and stochastic noise.

\subsection*{A stochastic credit operator defines when dendritic structure can help}

The preceding experiments show that a restricted route can discard useful
gradient components, but can also impose structure that improves learning.
To state this trade-off precisely, let the bold vector
\(\bm g=\nabla_{\bm w}\mathcal L\) denote the exact mean parameter gradient;
this is distinct from the scalar conductance \(g_i\). A mini-batch produces an
unbiased stochastic estimate
\(\widehat{\bm g}=\bm g+\bm\xi\), with
\(\mathbb E[\bm\xi]=0\) and covariance \(\Sigma\). We represent the feedback
available to the local rule by a linear credit operator \(M\):
\begin{equation}
\widetilde{\bm g}=M\widehat{\bm g},
\qquad
\bm w^+=\bm w-\eta\widetilde{\bm g}.
\label{eq:creditoperator}
\end{equation}
The operator combines the three restrictions defined above: which neuronal
coordinates are communicated, how those coordinates are assigned to
within-arbor routes and how route gains scale them. Exact backpropagation has
\(M=I\); a scalar broadcast, neuron-specific feedback and a \(K\)-route subtree
field correspond to progressively richer operators.

For an \(L\)-smooth loss, the expected loss after one routed stochastic update
obeys
\begin{equation}
\mathbb E\mathcal L(\bm w^+)\leq \mathcal L(\bm w)
-\eta\bm g^{\mathsf T}M\bm g
+\frac{L\eta^2}{2}\left(
\lVert M\bm g\rVert_2^2+\operatorname{tr}(M\Sigma M^{\mathsf T})\right).
\label{eq:creditoperatorbound}
\end{equation}
Each term has a direct interpretation. The inner product
\(\bm g^{\mathsf T}M\bm g\) is the task-gradient signal retained in a useful
direction. The norm \(\lVert M\bm g\rVert_2^2\) controls the finite-step cost of
that retained signal. The trace term is stochastic gradient noise admitted by
the same routes. If the retained signal is positively aligned with the exact
gradient, optimizing the bound over step size gives the operator utility
\begin{equation}
U(M)=\frac{[\bm g^{\mathsf T}M\bm g]^2}
{2L\{\lVert M\bm g\rVert_2^2+
\operatorname{tr}(M\Sigma M^{\mathsf T})\}} .
\label{eq:phaseutility}
\end{equation}
A restricted route can therefore improve a stochastic update only when the
noise it rejects compensates for the task signal it discards. It cannot
outperform exact full-batch backpropagation at matched first-order step norm.
The bound is exact for the corresponding quadratic loss and otherwise ranks
guaranteed, rather than necessarily realized, one-step progress. We use
\emph{field capture} for the fraction of the exact mean credit field retained
by a route projection; unlike \(U(M)\), capture alone does not include admitted
noise or route-gain error.

Equations~\ref{eq:creditoperatorbound} and \ref{eq:phaseutility} make four
testable predictions. A tree-structured route should become more useful as task
credit aligns with its span. Route resolution should have an interior optimum
when additional channels admit more noise than signal. Projection should help
only on the side of the signal--noise boundary where rejected noise exceeds
discarded signal. Finally, route-specific gains should help only when they are
matched to heterogeneous route reliability.

We tested these predictions in a fixed-state quadratic system with 16 credit
coordinates represented in the Haar basis of a balanced tree. Fifty independent
seeds generated 10,800 observations spanning route rank, task--tree alignment,
task hierarchy, route resolution, retained signal, admitted noise and branch
reliability. Because the forward representation was fixed, this experiment
isolates the geometry of stochastic credit; it is not a conductance-based
forward-neuron simulation. Here \(H\) denotes the number of statistically
relevant levels in the generated task, and \(D_{\mathrm r}\) denotes the number
of nested address levels available to feedback. Thus \(D_{\mathrm r}\) is
backward route resolution, not physical dendritic depth.

The alignment prediction held continuously. As task covariance rotated into
the nested-subtree basis, the route retained progressively more gradient
energy. At full alignment and \(K=4\), it captured 0.552 more energy than a
random rank-matched route (95\% paired interval 0.535--0.569; 50/50 seeds;
Fig.~\ref{fig:creditphase}b). It reached the rank-\(K\) Ky--Fan bound when its
span contained the leading covariance eigenspace. For a general covariance,
energy outside the route span gives an upper bound on the shortfall
(Supplementary Section~S4). The advantage was negligible under random
alignment and disappeared at full rank, where both route families represented
the complete space.

The resolution prediction also held. For generated task depths
\(H=1,2,3,4\), group-mean loss was minimized at
\(D_{\mathrm r}=H\) (Fig.~\ref{fig:creditphase}c). Averaged within seed across
the four task depths, matched resolution reduced loss by 0.0957 relative to the
best mismatch (48/50 seeds; \(P=3.00\times10^{-13}\)). This result validates
the crossover built into the controlled generator: routes that are too coarse
discard task signal, whereas routes that are too fine admit irrelevant noise.
It does not imply that arbitrary tasks have an optimum at
\(D_{\mathrm r}=H\).

Directly varying retained signal and retained noise recovered the analytical
projection boundary in all 25 condition means
(Fig.~\ref{fig:creditphase}d). Backpropagation followed by the same projection
outperformed unprojected stochastic backpropagation exactly when the projection
rejected more noise than signal. The routed local estimator remained below
this projected-backpropagation control, which has the same restricted span but
exact coefficients. Route-specific gain was equally conditional: when branch
signal-to-noise ratios differed, reliability-aligned gains outperformed the
best single global gain, whereas shuffled or reversed gains removed or reversed
the benefit (Fig.~\ref{fig:creditphase}e). A point model given the same gains
matched the routed model exactly. This backward reliability weighting parallels
the established conductance-based weighting of forward sensory cues
\citep{jordan2024conductance}.

We next asked whether positive shunting conductances could realize this gain
ordering. A compensating current held forward voltage fixed, isolating the
input-resistance contribution to backward transport. At maximum reliability
heterogeneity, aligned shunts improved one-step loss reduction by 0.001817
relative to the best global shunt (49/50 seeds) and improved final loss by
0.02040 (50/50). They did not, however, improve final loss relative to the
unshunted noisy rule (\(P=0.154\)). Shunts estimated from paired noisy local
observations also beat global or shuffled placement but remained worse than
unshunted learning. Positive conductance can therefore implement the predicted
relative gain ordering, but these experiments do not establish autonomous
shunt learning, superiority to no attenuation or a dendrite-exclusive
operation (Supplementary Figs.~S13 and S24).

Route span and coordinate conditioning are also distinct. Multiplying route
columns by fixed nonzero gains changed conditioning without changing their span
or field capture. Under noisy coefficient learning, Haar, nested-indicator and
gain-scaled coordinates showed different finite-data trajectories, but Gram
preconditioning made those trajectories identical
(Supplementary Fig.~S14). Conditioning affects how efficiently coefficients
are learned; it does not create additional address capacity.

Finally, we tested whether the one-step quantity \(U(M)\) predicts trained
behavior. Across 540 route conditions from the 2,700-fit factorial,
\(U(M)\) correlated with norm-matched one-step progress at \(\rho=0.937\) and
with final held-out accuracy at \(\rho=0.916\), more strongly than spectral
capture alone (Fig.~\ref{fig:creditphase}f). It located the mean route-depth
optimum for \(H=1,2,3\), but was conservative at the full-rank \(H=4\)
boundary and predicted the small trained \(K=4\) nested-subtree advantage in
only 6/20 seeds. The operator therefore predicts large contrasts and
within-family progress, but not every difference accumulated over a training
trajectory. The alignment--bandwidth plane in Fig.~\ref{fig:creditphase}g
summarizes this scope.

\subsection*{Task-aligned physical depth improves learning at fixed budgets}

The route depth \(D_{\mathrm r}\) in the preceding section describes the
resolution of a backward teaching field. A dendrite can also contribute
\emph{physical} depth by composing nonlinear operations in the forward
direction. We denote the number of serial forward stages by
\(D_{\mathrm p}\), displayed as D1--D4 for one through four stages. These two
notions of depth need not agree.

Generic increases in nominal dendritic depth impaired learning in the
regular-tree stress test, particularly under restricted feedback
(Supplementary Fig.~S4A). We therefore asked a narrower question: can serial
depth become useful when resources are matched and the order of divisive
stages mirrors the hierarchy of the task?

We isolated forward composition with a positive-rate divisive task
\citep{holt1997shunting,carandini2012normalization}. The target depended on
three nested multiplicative factors at fine, coarse and global scales. A serial
dendritic model could combine those factors in the same distal-to-proximal
order, whereas shuffled, reversed and independent sensor placements broke that
correspondence. D1--D3 models had the same eight nonsomatic branch units,
input contacts and 66,178 trainable parameters. The operating point was
calibrated before ten paired inferential seeds; the experiment is therefore a
mechanism-matched existence test, not an estimate of how frequently natural
tasks have this structure.

With exact backpropagation and aligned sensors, increasing physical depth from
D1 to D3 raised accuracy from 0.6096 to 0.9182, a 30.86-percentage-point gain
(Fig.~\ref{fig:physicaldepth}c,g). The gain disappeared when sensors were
independent (0.02 points), shuffled (\(-0.01\)) or reversed (\(-0.43\)).
An additive network became worse with depth, showing that additional stages
alone were insufficient; the benefit required ordered divisive composition.
A resource-identical grouped star remained near 0.610 and was 30.81 points
below the aligned serial tree at D3, whereas reversing sensor order removed
that difference (Fig.~\ref{fig:physicaldepth}f). More flexible
parameter-matched point networks exceeded the serial D3 tree by 6.79--7.20
points. Serial dendritic depth is therefore a constrained inductive bias, not
an absolute expressivity advantage over point networks.

We then asked whether the same forward benefit survived restricted local
credit. Repeating one somatic coordinate throughout the tree produced a
16.95-point D3-minus-D1 gain; supplying the exact path-resolved field increased
the gain to 27.90 points. Reversed sensor placement made both depth effects
negative (Fig.~\ref{fig:physicaldepth}d,g). Exact path gradients matched
automatic differentiation, whereas the shared-coordinate gradient cosine fell
from 0.946 at D1 to 0.767 at D3. When optimizer settings were matched,
soma-broadcast automatic differentiation reproduced the shared-coordinate
local rule, and adding path specificity recovered another 10.96 points
(Supplementary Fig.~S28). Thus physical depth can create useful forward
composition and, at the same time, make a shared teaching coordinate less
accurate.

The depth benefit required nearly complete task--sensor alignment. It remained
small through \(\alpha=0.75\) and increased sharply near
\(\alpha=1\) (Supplementary Fig.~S18). A literal grouped-point control replaced
serial inheritance with parameter-matched direct projections and remained
flat: the aligned H3 serial-minus-point difference was 30.87 points, but
reversed placement reduced it to \(-0.44\). These controls locate the benefit
in ordered composition rather than parameter count or sensor availability
alone.

Changing the number of task levels showed that useful depth saturates. For an
independent \(H=2\) task, aligned serial backpropagation gained 30.84 points
from D1 to D2, whereas reversed placement and the literal point control were
flat. For \(H=4\), D4 remained 25.63 points above D1 but was 1.46 points below
D3 (Fig.~\ref{fig:physicaldepthh4}b--e). Thus the trained optimum did not obey a
universal \(D_{\mathrm p}=H\) rule; the fourth physical stage added transport
and optimization costs without improving the representation. Re-execution
from the common H4 source reproduced the H2 and H3 effects
(Supplementary Fig.~S26).

We finally held physical depth at D3 and changed the task family. At full
alignment, serial backpropagation exceeded the literal grouped-point model by
30.32 points for nested multiplicative factors and 22.20 points for spatially
flattened factors, but by zero when each required ratio was already locally
available (Fig.~\ref{fig:physicaldepthh4}f,h). The exact-path local rule showed
the same ordering for nested and flattened tasks, but serial depth became
disadvantageous by 5.32 points for local ratios
(Fig.~\ref{fig:physicaldepthh4}g,h). Physical depth therefore helps only when
the forward task requires ordered composition; it can hurt when the relevant
operation is already local or when restricted credit cannot traverse the
additional stages.

\subsection*{Reconstructed arbors provide sparse, mostly coarse route capacity}

The controlled trees establish what a nested address could do. We next asked
whether reconstructed arbors actually provide such addresses at useful
bandwidth. This analysis measures \emph{structural capacity}: how well a small
dictionary of subtree routes represents modeled compartment fields on measured
anatomy. It does not measure a teaching signal in the animal. The question is
motivated by connectomic evidence that anatomy constrains candidate learning
routes \citep{kornfeld2020anatomical,weis2025morphology}.

The primary cohort contained eight reconstructed layer 2--5
intratelencephalic neurons from one MICrONS mouse and 28,669 mapped inputs after
cable compression. Every branch point defined a binary route containing all
descendant inputs. We compared these nested-subtree routes with a dense
principal-component ceiling and with controls matched for route count or coarse
geometry: random real paths, cable-depth bins, shuffled subtree assignment and
degree--depth surrogate trees.

With eight channels, nested-subtree routes captured 0.487 of a model-matched
perturbation field, compared with 0.285 for random paths, 0.222 for depth bins
and 0.198 after subtree shuffling. To remove circularity, we generated a second
field from the reciprocal cable rather than from the route kernel. The ordering
persisted: capture was 0.696 for nested subtrees, 0.347 for random paths, 0.447
for depth bins and 0.308 after shuffling (Fig.~\ref{fig:topology}c,d; route
support and direct-type controls in Supplementary Fig.~S20). Degree--depth surrogate trees recovered much of
the effect, however, showing that most capacity came from coarse depth and
branching structure; the increment attributable to fine ancestry depended on
the field generator.

The main structural advantage was economical wiring. At eight channels,
nested-subtree routes retained 85.0\% of dense-oracle capture while using
6.9\% of the dense feedback connections (Fig.~\ref{fig:topology}e). Relative
to the dense oracle, this is a 14.2-fold increase in capture per connection.
The more informative anatomy-specific comparison is the 2.7-fold advantage
over a density-matched shuffled dictionary (Fig.~\ref{fig:topology}f).
These ratios count feedback connections; they do not estimate cable length,
metabolic cost or connection reliability.

Two additional cohorts tested robustness. A disjoint 47-cell
\texttt{minnie65} cohort reproduced the route ordering (Supplementary
Fig.~S10). We then applied the
same pipeline to a 12-cell Pinky v185 cohort from a second MICrONS mouse; ten
cells passed the predefined quality criteria. At four channels, nested-subtree
capture exceeded random, depth-bin and shuffled controls in all ten cells
(Fig.~\ref{fig:topology}g). The two animals therefore agree in the direction of
model-matched structural capacity, but \(n=2\) animals supports only
descriptive animal-level replication (Supplementary Fig.~S27).

A tree-Haar decomposition further asked whether irregular arbors provide the
coarse-to-fine hierarchy used in the theory. In the 47-cell cohort, coarse
modes contained 0.407 of non-scalar route-field energy while occupying 0.192
of dimensions, a 2.35-fold enrichment over isotropic coordinate noise.
Compared with a column-permuted subtree control, however, the excess was only
0.0345 and its interval included zero (\(P=0.078\); Supplementary Fig.~S25).
Real arbors are multiscale, but this analysis did not establish enrichment
specific to their fine topology.

Finally, we asked whether inhibitory contacts exhibit the spatial organization
required to regulate these routes. Twenty target cells were joined to the
synapse-resolved MICrONS inhibitory census
\citep{lovettbarron2012regulation,vogels2011inhibitory,
schneidermizell2025inhibitory}. Contacts from the same inhibitory axon were
closer along the dendritic tree and overlapped descendant domains more than
path-distance-matched sites. Only the distance effect survived aggregation
within 92 presynaptic axons, and matching jointly for path distance and
three-dimensional proximity removed all three effects. Distal-targeting
classes addressed smaller descendant domains than perisomatic classes
(Supplementary Fig.~S12). These observations support class-dependent spatial
scales and local co-targeting, but not a geometry-independent teaching-route
organization.

\subsection*{Shunting regulates route gain only in permissive electrotonic regimes}

The regular-tree foundation experiments showed that feedback bandwidth,
teaching noise, inhibition and nominal depth interact strongly, but those
sweeps did not isolate conductance-specific error transport from changes in
forward state and optimization, and some historical noise-task shunting
conditions fall outside the positive-conductance input-validity criterion
used here (Supplementary Figs.~S1--S4). We therefore treat them as regime and
robustness boundaries; the current-matched focal intervention below is the
direct test of conductance-dependent adjoint transport.

An anatomical subtree is a candidate address; it becomes a gain-control route
only if a local conductance change affects credit preferentially within that
subtree. We therefore compared a focal inhibitory conductance with an additive
current chosen to produce the same local voltage change. The two perturbations
match the forward effect at the intervention site, but only the conductance
changes the transport operator.

For a passive conductance matrix \(G\), let
\(\bm q=G^{-1}\bm e_s\,\partial\mathcal L/\partial V_s\) be the adjoint of a
somatic loss. Adding a focal shunt \(\kappa_k\) at compartment \(k\) gives, by
the Sherman--Morrison identity,
\begin{equation}
\bm q'=\bm q-
\frac{\kappa_k q_k}{1+\kappa_k(G^{-1})_{kk}}G^{-1}\bm e_k .
\label{eq:sherman}
\end{equation}
The scalar prefactor sets the magnitude of the change, whereas the
Green's-function column \(\bm c_k=G^{-1}\bm e_k\) determines where that change
spreads. We quantify descendant selectivity by
\begin{equation}
S_k=\frac{\operatorname{median}_{i\in D(k)}|c_{k,i}|}
{\operatorname{median}_{j\in C(k)}|c_{k,j}|}>1,
\label{eq:selectivitycriterion}
\end{equation}
where \(D(k)\) contains descendants and \(C(k)\) depth-matched off-route
sites. Thus \(S_k>1\) means that the focal Green's-function column is larger
below the perturbed branch than at depth-matched sites elsewhere. This is a
condition for spatially selective transport, not a claim that inhibition
computes the error itself \citep{gidon2012inhibition}.

In the normalized proxy model, 101 focal sites across eight reconstructed cells
showed greater descendant localization after shunting than after the
current-matched additive perturbation. The paired difference was 0.069 log
units (95\% cell-bootstrap interval 0.053--0.086; 8/8 cells;
Fig.~\ref{fig:focal}b,c). Restoring the baseline adjoint while holding the
post-shunt voltage field fixed removed 0.079 of localization. A two-factor
Shapley decomposition likewise attributed most of the contrast to altered
adjoint transport rather than to the synaptic driving force. A disjoint
45-cell v661 cohort showed the same direction (Supplementary Fig.~S10). These controls identify a
conductance-specific change in the modeled gradient at matched somatic state.

The effect was not present at standard passive cable parameters. At
\(R_a=150\,\Omega\,\mathrm{cm}\) and the textbook passive value
\(R_m=15{,}000\,\Omega\,\mathrm{cm}^2\), the shunt-minus-additive contrast was
\(-0.0019\) in the original cohort (2/8 cells positive) and 0.0024 in v661,
both effectively null. Localization emerged when membrane resistance was
lowered or distributed background conductance was added
(Fig.~\ref{fig:focal}f), consistent with high-conductance in vivo regimes
\citep{chance2002gain,destexhe2003highconductance}. The boundary is governed by
the focal Green's-function column and electrotonic length scale; a global
inverse-operator norm bound cannot predict spatial selectivity.

We then added weak voltage-dependent Na, K, Ca, HCN and NMDA currents and
recomputed the exact local Jacobian at each steady state. All 512 channel-density
draws across the eight cells converged in the permissive conductance regime. At
unit normalized dose, mean localization was 0.529 and exceeded the additive
control by 0.382 (8/8 cells); every modeled descendant gradient was attenuated
without a sign reversal (Fig.~\ref{fig:focal}e,g). The active extension shows
that the local sensitivity can survive voltage-dependent conductances in this
regime. It neither simulates channel kinetics nor rescues the null at the
standard passive calibration, and it does not show that inhibitory synapses
carry teaching signals in vivo.

\subsection*{Measured responses do not reveal morphology-specific credit alignment}

The preceding anatomical and perturbation analyses establish route capacity
and a possible gain mechanism using modeled credit fields. They do not show
that biological activity supplies credit in those routes. To test that next
level of the claim, we paired reconstructed MICrONS arbors with repeated visual
responses and constructed task gradients from the measured inputs and outputs.
Seven target cells contributed 13 eligible imaging scans, each with 136
repeated visual conditions.

We first asked whether inputs on the same subtree had more similar functional
responses than equally separated inputs on different subtrees. After
controlling Euclidean distance and dendritic depth, the mean target-level
partial rank effect was \(-0.069\) in the one-scan cohort (95\%
target-bootstrap interval \(-0.255\) to 0.108; 3/7 targets positive) and
\(-0.048\) after all eligible scans were averaged within target
(\(-0.224\) to 0.118; Fig.~\ref{fig:boundary}b). Thus, this within-arbor test
did not detect functional clustering by shared ancestry. It addresses a
different scale from the broader like-to-like wiring reported in the same
volume \citep{ding2025wiring}.

A coarse major-branch model was uninformative because the measured task was
nearly rank one: a single dense channel captured 0.965 of the response-derived
credit energy (effective rank 1.07), whereas the tested feedback budget was
four channels. As expected under this rank saturation, nested-subtree routes
did not improve capture or learning over depth-bin, scalar or shuffled routes
(Supplementary Fig.~S22). We therefore repeated the test on the complete
compressed reconstruction of each target cell, where routes can distinguish
locations within a major branch.

In the complete-tree model, every imaged partner supplied an excitatory
conductance at its mapped segment. Exact learning used the trial-specific
soma-to-segment adjoint. Each restricted rule instead combined scalar output
error and local eligibility with a fixed four-channel route vector. Ten
held-out-stimulus splits across all 13 scans produced 520 fits, which we
averaged within scan and then within target. Mean normalized held-out MSE was
0.788 with exact compartment errors, 0.832 with nested-subtree routes, 0.833
with random anatomical routes and 0.839 with site-shuffled routes
(Fig.~\ref{fig:boundary}a). The nested-subtree improvement was 0.0070 over
site shuffling (95\% interval \(-0.0207\) to 0.0321) and 0.0013 over random
routes (\(-0.0090\) to 0.0113). Nested-subtree capture at the exact-learning
checkpoint was also below both controls. The measured-response objective
therefore provided no evidence that the available ancestry routes were
preferentially used.

The phase theory makes a specific conditional prediction: a fixed route
dictionary should become useful when task credit is rotated into its span. On
each reconstructed tree, we therefore varied only the direction of a
fixed-energy quadratic task field between the nested-subtree span and its
orthogonal complement; channel count, curvature and dictionary were unchanged
(Fig.~\ref{fig:boundary}c). Nested-subtree capture increased from 0 to 1. At
40\% alignment it captured 0.400 of field energy and achieved 0.615 of the
norm-matched full-gradient loss reduction. It lost to every control at
orthogonality and exceeded every control at complete alignment in all eight
cells (Fig.~\ref{fig:boundary}d). This controlled rescue establishes
sufficiency of alignment for the model. Because it uses imposed fields and
oracle projection coefficients, it does not establish endogenous route use
(Supplementary Fig.~S5).

As an external comparison at the neuron-coordinate level, we reanalyzed the
six-animal causal P+/P- brain--computer-interface experiment of Francioni et
al. \citep{francioni2026vectorized}. Its published mapping assigns opposite
signs to two selected neurons. In a signed-versus-common coordinate basis, the
error-reduction-minus-error-increase contrast separated P+ from P- in all six
animals (mean 0.2251). The signed mode contained 83.7\% of the pooled squared
projection energy (95\% animal-bootstrap interval 60.6--95.7\%;
Fig.~\ref{fig:boundary}e,f). This retrospective re-expression is consistent
with neuron-specific teaching coordinates, but it is not an independent test
of those coordinates and says nothing about within-arbor routing
(Supplementary Fig.~S17).

The combined results occupy the alignment--bandwidth plane introduced in
Fig.~\ref{fig:creditphase}g. A scalar signal loses neuron identity; full-rank
feedback removes differences between equal spans; and intermediate-bandwidth
routes help only when task credit is sufficiently aligned with their span.
The measured-response null, the trained benefit at $K=4$ and the controlled
alignment rescue fall in different regions of this same conditional map.

\section*{Discussion}

Our results give a conditional answer to whether dendrites can help local
learning. The exact gradient of a conductance-based tree factors into a
synapse-local eligibility term and a compartment-specific error transported
through the arbor. Dendritic structure does not create the error signal and is
not required to represent the same computation. Instead, it can provide a
wiring-efficient set of addresses for distributing a neuron-specific teaching
signal, while the conductance state regulates the gain of each route. Whether
those resources help is determined by their alignment with the task and by the
noise they admit.

The stochastic credit operator makes that statement quantitative. Its utility
\(U(M)\) balances retained gradient signal against finite-step distortion and
transported noise. This balance predicts the main within-family progress
effects and locates changes of regime that capture alone misses. It also
defines the scope of the claim. A projected or restricted stochastic rule can
outperform another noisy, bandwidth-limited rule, but it cannot outperform an
exact full-batch gradient at matched first-order step norm. Small advantages
that accumulate over training trajectories, including the \(K=4\)
nested-subtree effect, are not themselves one-step predictions. The relevant
upper control is exact backpropagation followed by the same projection.

The experiments separate two questions that are often conflated. The first is
how a circuit selects the correct neuron. Replacing a scalar broadcast with one
teaching coordinate per neuron recovers most of the learning deficit across
MNIST, Fashion-MNIST and the prospective depth cohorts. A flattened CIFAR-10
control reproduces the same feedback-information ordering ($29.7\%$ under
scalar feedback, $35.0\%$ at rank four, $47.2\%$ with exact transport and
$49.5\%$ for backpropagation), although without a convolutional front end or
augmentation it is a generality check rather than a vision benchmark
(Supplementary Fig.~S4C). The second is how that
coordinate is distributed within the selected neuron. Correct ownership of
the coordinate adds a smaller benefit, and nested-subtree addresses help only
at intermediate bandwidth when task credit varies on the scale those routes
resolve: they lose to unrestricted low-rank routes at the smallest budgets,
exceed the best matched non-anatomical control by 1.3 percentage points at
\(K=4\), and become equivalent at full rank. Point, flat, grouped-point and
explicitly gated implementations agree when given the same routed field. Thus,
the advantage is a property of the feedback operator and its wiring cost, not
of dendritic material alone.

This comparison also clarifies the relation to backpropagation. Backpropagation
computes the exact nonlocal error at every node; a local rule replaces that
field with signals physically available at the synapse. Dendritic routes can
reduce the approximation error of that replacement, but only after a circuit
has supplied neuron-specific credit. They do not remove the need to explain
where teaching coordinates come from. In this sense, dendritic routing and
feedback alignment address complementary parts of the same problem: the former
specifies a within-neuron basis, whereas the latter concerns how an approximate
feedback pathway becomes aligned with the forward computation.

Physical dendritic depth plays a different, forward role. At fixed branch,
contact and parameter budgets, serial divisive stages improve learning when
their order matches a multiplicative task hierarchy; independent, shuffled or
reversed sensor placement removes or reverses the benefit. The effect
replicates for \(H=2\) and \(H=3\), saturates at D3 in the \(H=4\) extension,
and disappears under backpropagation when the required ratio is already
locally available. Literal grouped-point and grouped-star controls isolate
serial composition, whereas more flexible parameter-matched point networks
outperform the tree. Physical depth is therefore a structured inductive bias
under a calibrated task--sensor match, not a general expressivity advantage.
It can also increase credit-transport cost: a shared somatic coordinate becomes
less aligned as branch states differentiate, whereas exact path transport
recovers the compartment field computed by backpropagation.

Reconstructed arbors provide a plausible circuit-economic implementation of
such addresses. In modeled fields on measured MICrONS anatomy, nested-subtree
routes retain most dense-oracle capture using about \(7\%\) of its feedback
connections. The \(14.2\)-fold dense-normalized capture-per-connection value
must be paired with the anatomy-specific \(2.7\)-fold advantage over a
density-matched shuffled control. A second MICrONS animal reproduces the
direction of this model-matched capacity result. Fine topology, however, does
not reliably outperform controls matched for depth and parent degree, and the
inhibitory-census effects weaken under axon-level aggregation and joint
geometric matching. These analyses establish available sparse, mostly coarse
addresses; they do not establish their use during learning.

Focal shunting likewise has a specific role. A conductance perturbation changes
the Green's-function adjoint along every path through a compartment, whereas a
current-matched additive perturbation does not change the transport operator.
Descendant-enriched attenuation is strong in normalized and active
high-conductance models but is effectively null at the textbook passive
calibration (\(R_m=15{,}000\,\Omega\,\mathrm{cm}^2\)). This state dependence is
consistent with high-conductance gain modulation
\citep{chance2002gain,destexhe2003highconductance} and spatial inhibitory
domains \citep{gidon2012inhibition}. Reliability-aligned shunts outperform
global or misplaced attenuation when branch noise is heterogeneous, yet
neither fixed nor adaptively estimated shunts improve final loss over
unshunted noisy learning here. An explicit point gate matches the operation.
This backward route-gain question differs from forward gain--load robustness
\citep{safaai2026gainload} and from circuits in which inhibition computes an
error \citep{rossbroich2023disinhibitory,galloni2026cellular,greedy2026celltype}.

The functional analyses mark the biological boundary. Neither the coarse
response surrogate nor the complete-tree experiment detects reliable
morphology-specific alignment with response-derived task gradients in the
analyzed MICrONS cohort. Rotating an otherwise fixed task field into the same
anatomical span rescues capture and optimization, demonstrating controlled
sufficiency without endogenous use. The six-animal Francioni reanalysis places
the published vectorized instructive signal in a signed-versus-common basis; it
is consistent with neuron-specific coordinates but is not an independent test
of their existence or within-arbor transport. Population-manifold constraints
provide a related alignment principle at a different scale
\citep{sadtler2014constraints}.

This framing distinguishes the contribution from neighboring work. Dendritic
nonlinearities expand forward single-neuron computation
\citep{poirazi2003pyramidal,london2005dendritic,branco2010single,chavlis2025dendrites},
whereas the present theory asks how limited feedback is routed. Segregated,
predictive and burst-based models deliver teaching signals to one or a few
compartments \citep{urbanczik2014dendritic,guerguiev2017segregated,
sacramento2018dendritic,payeur2021burst}; here the object is the address span of
a complete arbor. Most closely, detailed-arbor optimization uses cable
sensitivities to train a single neuron's input--output function
\citep{bicknell2021synaptic}; here circuit-derived error defines a compartment
field that must be compressed under bandwidth, alignment and noise
constraints. Engineered active-segment and gating systems demonstrate useful
routing designs \citep{sezener2021gated,iyer2022activedendrites,lv2025dendritic}.
Concurrent preprints on developmental teaching alignment and a signed
connectomic error motif provide complementary circuit-level precedents
\citep{meier2026criticalperiod,penafernandez2026signedxor}.

The framework yields a branch-resolved prediction. After matching somatic
voltage, output and global error, focal dendrite-targeting inhibition during
learning should alter subsequent plasticity more strongly below the perturbed
branch than at electrotonically matched sites on sister subtrees. The contrast
should scale with perturbation conductance and the focal Green's-function
column, strengthen in permissive conductance states, and not be reproduced by a
current-matched additive perturbation. Dendritic recording, focal inhibition
and longitudinal spine measurements could separate credit routing from forward
gain control \citep{francioni2026vectorized,cichon2015branch,bloss2016structured}.

The scope remains limited. The theory treats steady-state, non-spiking trees;
reconstructed-cell analyses are sensitivity models rather than physiological
fits; the principal anatomy, inhibitory and functional cohorts come from one
mouse; and the second animal tests only model-matched structural capacity. The
functional sample contains seven targets, route coefficients are oracles, and
the external six-animal analysis is retrospective. Synthetic route and depth
tasks are mechanism-matched existence tests, not estimates of prevalence in
natural learning. The active extension linearizes steady states rather than
simulating channel kinetics, and reliability experiments require paired noisy
observations or oracle factors plus a state clamp. Temporal eligibility,
recurrent paths, spikes and plateau-mediated or behavioral-timescale
plasticity remain outside the model
\citep{bittner2017btsp,magee2020plasticity}.

Within these limits, dendritic structure plays a positive but conditional role
in local learning. Neuron-specific coordinates provide most useful feedback;
nested subtrees offer wiring-efficient addresses; conductance regulates their
gain; and task alignment determines whether these resources improve learning
or merely add bias, noise and transport cost.

\section*{Methods}

The methods follow the hierarchy used in the Results. We first define the
conductance-tree forward model and its local update, then describe the
controlled simulations of feedback coordinate, within-arbor address and route
gain, and finally describe the reconstructed-arbor and measured-response
analyses. Throughout, $D_{\mathrm p}$ denotes the number of serial dendritic
stages in the forward model and $D_{\mathrm r}$ denotes the depth resolved by
the feedback dictionary. Display labels D1--D4 refer to
$D_{\mathrm p}=1,\ldots,4$; they are architecture labels, not neuronal cell
types.

\subsection*{Directed conductance-tree network}

Each compartment implemented the current balance in
Eq.~\ref{eq:directedcurrentbalance}. Because coupling was directed from child
to parent, a forward pass evaluated the tree from terminal compartments to the
soma and directly produced the steady state in Eq.~\ref{eq:directedvoltage}; no
iterative equilibrium solver was required for these controlled networks.
Excitatory and inhibitory reversal potentials were fixed within each
experiment. Raw trainable parameters were mapped monotonically to nonnegative
conductances, and a compartment could apply the branch-output nonlinearity
$a_n=f_n(V_n)$ before transmission. The implementation calls this operation
reactivation; $f_n$ was the identity when it was disabled. Supplementary
Section~S1 specifies the parameter transform, complete derivatives and update
order. We checked Eqs.~\ref{eq:factorization} and \ref{eq:pathgain} against
PyTorch automatic differentiation on fixed batches and checkpoints. Parameter
tensors, rather than individual elements, were the units of the reconstruction
diagnostic.

\subsection*{Local learning and feedback fields}

For a mini-batch of size $B$, the implemented three-factor gradient estimate
was
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\nabla}_{g_i}\mathcal L
=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}
x_i^{(b)}R_n^{\mathrm{tot},(b)}
\bigl(E_i-V_n^{(b)}\bigr)\widehat\delta_n^{(b)}.
\end{equation}
The tested fields $\widehat\delta_n$ included a global scalar broadcast, the
width-matched scalar implementation used by the released code,
neuron-specific sharing, low-rank random feedback, selected subtree routes and
exact path transport. A neuron-specific field repeats one somatic coordinate
across all descendants of that soma; it does not provide an independent error
to every compartment.
When $\widehat\delta_n=\partial\mathcal L/\partial V_n$, the three-factor rule
equals the exact conductance gradient. The exact-transport experiment therefore
separates feedback approximation from local eligibility. Four- and five-factor
variants multiply the three-factor update by bounded, slowly estimated
stage-level reliability terms. We treat these terms as preconditioners rather
than theoretically required teaching signals.

Regular-tree feedback comparisons used MNIST classification with matched
$[3,3]$ morphologies, optimizers, decoders, schedules and paired
initializations within each architecture. The complete feedback ladder crossed
strict scalar broadcast, neuron-specific feedback and exact path transport with
two architectures and 15 independently initialized seeds, yielding 90 fits.
In the strict-scalar arm, the decoder-derived somatic error was reduced to one
signed value per example and that value was used at every local-rule stage.
Every run was required to contain its resolved configuration, finite held-out
metric and final checkpoint, and W\&B was disabled. The three fields used
identical executable source files and Python environments, and their resolved
scientific configurations were identical after removing seed and feedback
mode. The separate exact-transport
rule-by-decoder implementation check used five matched shunting seeds per
condition. Test data remained hidden during checkpoint selection.
Supplementary Section~S5 and the accompanying machine-readable configurations
report the full hyperparameters and provenance.

We also audited the earlier legacy \texttt{per\_soma} scalar-fallback arm. In
that implementation, the 128-dimensional somatic error was retained only at
the 128-wide somatic stage and was reduced to one signed scalar at the 384- and
1,152-wide dendritic stages. Thus every feedforward synaptic update---spanning
2,408,448 candidate parameters and 92,160 active contacts per network---used
scalar feedback. Neuron-specific coordinates reached only 384 final
branch-to-soma couplings and 256 somatic gain and threshold parameters (640 of
2,413,312 core parameters, 0.027\%). A separate 30-fit strict-scalar control
tested whether this near-somatic exception affected the reported accuracy
contrast. Before inspecting its outcomes, we froze a practical-equivalence
margin of $\pm0.5$ percentage points and required the 95\% paired-seed
bootstrap interval to lie wholly inside that margin; the main-figure rule used
strict scalar regardless of this audit's direction.

The neuron-specific and legacy scalar-fallback arms re-executed the released
implementation with the width- and connectivity-matched additive architecture.
Relative to the archived conference cohort, their four condition means shifted
by at most 0.20 percentage points. Figure~\ref{fig:feedback}b and all primary
MNIST contrasts instead use the separately executed strict-scalar arm together
with the matched neuron-specific and exact-path arms; the legacy values are
retained only for the implementation audit \citep{safaai2026localcredit}.

The Fashion-MNIST replication changed only the dataset. It retained the same
regular $[3,3]$ architecture, 128 somata, contact counts, three-factor rule,
local decoder, architecture-specific initialization, optimizer and
validation-based checkpoint selection. Images were flattened to 784 inputs
without normalization or augmentation. For each fresh seed 10200--10209, the
60,000-image training set was divided into 48,000 training and 12,000
validation examples. The official 10,000-image test set was evaluated only
after checkpoint selection. These seed labels are also used by the unrelated
physical-depth cohort, but the experiments are independent.

Sixty fits crossed two architectures with the matched-width/scalar-fallback
field, neuron-specific feedback and exact path transport. The primary
replication criterion required the paired neuron-specific-minus-fallback
interval to exclude zero and at least
8/10 seed differences to be positive in each architecture. Intervals used
50,000 paired-seed bootstrap draws, and exact two-sided sign-flip tests
enumerated all $2^{10}$ assignments. Quality-control criteria required
complete finite results and checkpoints, the specified seeds and no fallback
warning; every run passed. A concurrent source edit divided each architecture
across two source manifests. The only code difference was an optional rule
override that no resolved configuration enabled; both manifests are retained
with the audit record.

\subsection*{Regular-tree regime and one-step analyses}

The task, inhibition-dose, depth, broadcast-noise, rule-family, error-source,
mechanism-control, feedback-ladder and CIFAR-10 analyses reuse the source
tables and configurations from the original regular-tree study. Unless noted
otherwise, each condition contains five independent training seeds. The
three-factor, four-factor and five-factor comparison, together with the
local-mismatch control, contains three seeds and is treated descriptively.
Supplementary Section~S5 describes the synthetic context-gating and
noise-resilience classification tasks. CIFAR-10 images were flattened, with no
convolutional front end, data augmentation or large-scale benchmark claim.

The prospective depth-by-feedback cohort was specified before outcomes were
inspected. It crossed two tasks, two neuron models, four dendritic depths and
three local feedback fields (the legacy matched-width/scalar fallback,
neuron-specific sharing and exact path transport) at ten seeds. One matched
backpropagation model was
also trained for every task--core--depth--seed combination, yielding 480
local-learning and 160 backpropagation runs. All local conditions used the
factorization-derived three-factor rule.

Configurations fixed the decoder, optimizer, training horizon, reactivation
initialization policy, 40 excitatory and 20 inhibitory contacts per distal
branch, and one population of 128 modeled neurons. The single-population
design prevented equal neuron indices in different feedforward populations
from sharing a teaching coordinate. Because nominal depth changed both
compartment and parameter counts, this cohort was treated as a depth stress
test rather than a capacity-matched comparison.

Each analyzed run had to include its resolved configuration, seed record,
checkpoint, complete learning curves, resource record and finite held-out
metrics. Quality control also required every expected dendritic stage and
excluded critical log errors, nonfinite parameters, degenerate coupling stages
and transport-shape fallbacks. A subsequent outcome-independent input-validity
rule excluded 160 synthetic-noise shunting runs because a signed transfer
output cannot parameterize positive conductances. This left 480 eligible
runs. Condition means used ten paired seeds. Paired
contrasts used 20,000 bootstrap resamples and exact two-sided Wilcoxon tests
computed from the ten seed differences.

The bandwidth-matched routing cohort was specified separately. It crossed the two
tasks and neuron models at depths two and four, with either correct
neuron-specific routing or a fixed derangement, across seeds 42--51. The
derangement maps every somatic coordinate to a different neuron and has no
fixed point. It preserves the number and per-example distribution of teaching
coordinates, the forward network and all training settings. The same validity
rule removed 40 synthetic-noise shunting runs, leaving 120 runs in six
task--model--depth conditions. Correct-minus-deranged accuracy was paired by
seed. We report paired bootstrap intervals, exact two-sided Wilcoxon tests and
Benjamini--Hochberg correction across the six retained contrasts.

\subsection*{Trained within-neuron subtree-address experiment}

The two-stream support-task configuration, task parameters, ten analysis seeds
and quality-control criteria were specified before the preliminary validation
seed was inspected.
Each example contained two 12-dimensional sibling-stream inputs, a binary
context and a balanced binary label. In the context-selected stream, the input
mean was the label sign multiplied by a seed-specific unit teacher vector. The
other stream had the opposite sign at 0.75 times that amplitude. Independent
Gaussian noise with standard deviation 1.15 was then added. Every training and
test split contained identical counts for all four context--label
combinations. Context therefore had zero correlation with label, both streams
were active, and the inactive stream was a structured distractor rather than a
zero mask.

For branch weights $\bm w_A,\bm w_B$, the somatic logit was
\begin{equation}
z=\mathbb 1[c=0]\bm w_A^{\mathsf T}\bm x_A+
\mathbb 1[c=1]\bm w_B^{\mathsf T}\bm x_B.
\end{equation}
All conditions shared the paired initialization, mini-batches and forward
model. The one-neuron and depth-shared fields applied the logistic output error
to both branches. Correct $K=2$ feedback applied the error only to the selected
sibling, whereas fixed derangement applied it to the other sibling. The random
rank-two control rotated the two context coordinates using a seed-fixed
orthogonal matrix. Exact transport and analytic backpropagation used the
selected-branch derivative. The gated-point emulation used the same two
parameter groups and context-gated input eligibility, providing the explicit
point-model resource needed to reproduce the dendritic address.

Each seed used 2,048 training examples and 2,048 independent test examples.
Mini-batch SGD used batch size 64, learning rate 0.3 and 60 epochs, with
Gaussian weight initialization of standard deviation 0.03. Switch interference
was measured after 12 additional epochs on 1,024 balanced-label examples from
context 1 only, without resetting the optimizer or weights. Gradient cosine
and scaled capture used concatenated per-example parameter gradients. For the
one-step analysis, each candidate batch gradient was norm matched to the exact
gradient before a fixed step of 0.25 was applied.

\subsection*{Credit-operator signal--noise experiment and factorial reanalysis}

The signal--noise experiment used 16 Euclidean coordinates represented in the
orthonormal Haar basis of a balanced binary tree, ordered from the global mode
(level 0) to individual sibling contrasts (level 4). The configuration,
50 analysis seeds 7000--7049, two preliminary validation seeds and numerical
tolerances were specified before the analysis outcomes were inspected. The
preliminary runs verified basis orthogonality, projector idempotence,
static-gain span invariance and exact agreement with the explicit point gate.
Execution required unchanged SHA-256 hashes for the configuration, runner and
numerical reference. Every expected row was present: 3,750 spectral, 1,800
depth, 3,750 projection and 1,500 reliability rows.

In the spectral screen, task covariance assigned total energy
$(0.15,0.35,0.30,0.20,0)$ to tree levels 0--4. An alignment coefficient
$\rho\in\{0,0.25,0.5,0.75,1\}$ mixed this covariance with an independently
rotated covariance having the same eigenvalues. Ancestry, random rank-matched
and dense principal-eigenspace projectors were compared at
$K\in\{1,2,4,8,16\}$. The dense value was also calculated as the Ky--Fan sum
of the leading $K$ eigenvalues divided by total covariance energy. Because the
endpoint covariances had equal trace, ancestry-minus-random capture varied
affinely with alignment. Endpoint differences therefore gave the exact
per-seed crossover; seeds already favoring ancestry at zero alignment were
assigned threshold zero. Capture was $\tr(P\Sigma_g)/\tr(\Sigma_g)$.

The same-span diagnostic compared raw nested indicators, their tree-Haar basis
and indicators multiplied by fixed nonzero lognormal column gains. Rank,
coherence, positive-spectrum Gram condition number and capture were calculated
directly.

In the depth screen, a unit-norm target was sampled from modes at levels
$0$ through $H$, with $H\in\{1,2,3,4\}$. The gradient-noise standard deviation
at level $\ell$ was $0.65[1+1.5\max(\ell-H,0)]$. Starting from zero, 80
common-noise updates of size 0.12 used the projector onto levels $0$ through
$D_{\mathrm r}\in\{1,2,3,4\}$. A fixed leaf permutation provided the rewired
control, and the identity provided stochastic backpropagation.

The projection screen independently set retained signal $r$ and retained noise
$n$ to $\{0.25,0.5,0.75,0.9,1\}$. It compared an unprojected stochastic
gradient, backpropagation followed by the same projection, and a routed
estimator with an added total coefficient-noise fraction of 0.05. For a unit
step on the identity-curvature quadratic, expected losses were calculated
analytically and stochastic losses were recorded from paired draws.

In the reliability screen, eight disjoint blocks had signal and noise energies
proportional to $\exp(hx_b)$ and $\exp(-hx_b)$, respectively, with equally
spaced $x_b\in[-1,1]$ and heterogeneity
$h\in\{0,0.5,1,1.5,2\}$. We compared no attenuation, the best common gain,
$S_b/(S_b+N_b)$, seed-shuffled gains, reversed gains and a point gate receiving
the same branch factors. Intervals used 20,000 resamples of the 50 paired
seeds, and paired tests were two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests.

The secondary analysis reconstructed the frozen training data,
initialization and route matrices for all 2,700 completed subtree-factorial
fits. For each of 20 seeds, 64 independently sampled minibatches of size 64
estimated gradient-noise energy at initialization. The exact full-data gradient
provided the signal term, the largest logistic Hessian eigenvalue provided
local smoothness, and Eq.~\ref{eq:phaseutility} provided utility. Correlations
were restricted to the 540 dendritic-tree route conditions, and uncertainty
resampled whole seed blocks. This is an initialization diagnostic of an
existing experiment, not a second confirmatory training cohort.

\subsection*{Positive-rate nonlinear physical-depth experiment}

To connect the fixed-state hierarchy result to the original rate-based
network, we used the registered
\texttt{hierarchical\_\allowbreak gain\_\allowbreak load} generator and the
production population-network implementation, DendriNet. Inputs were ordered
as nonnegative excitatory and inhibitory rate streams. For binary label $y$,
the distal excitatory signal had the schematic form
\begin{equation}
E_j=(e_0+y\Delta+\epsilon_j)
\exp\!\left\{\sum_{\ell=1}^{3}\sigma z_{\ell,a_\ell(j)}
-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{3}\sigma^2\right\},
\end{equation}
where $a_\ell(j)$ indexes the fine, coarse or global latent factor containing
coordinate $j$. Three disjoint inhibitory blocks measured these factors
separately. At sensor alignment $\alpha$, their log gains were
$\sigma[\alpha z_{\ell,a_\ell(j)}+
\sqrt{1-\alpha^2}z'_{\ell,a_\ell(j)}]$. Thus, $\alpha=1$ supplied matched
branch-local nuisance sensors, whereas $\alpha=0$ preserved the complete
factor marginals but made the sensors independent. A trial-shuffled control
cyclically permuted the sensor field after class balancing, preserving its
marginal distribution while removing example-wise pairing.

At every physical stage, the production shunting equation was
\begin{equation}
V_n=\frac{E_n+C_n}
{1+E_n+I_n+G_n+\epsilon},
\end{equation}
where $C_n$ and $G_n$ denote child current and child conductance. All rate and
conductance terms were nonnegative. Reactivation was the identity, making the
divisive branch equation the only intermediate nonlinearity. D1 $[8]$, D2
$[2,3]$ and D3 $[2,1,2]$ contained one, two and three nonsomatic stages,
respectively. Each architecture retained eight nonsomatic branch units per
soma and the same 4/2/2 fine/coarse/proximal sensor inventory. Every model had
64 somata, 16 excitatory and 12 inhibitory contacts per branch, 14,336 active
synapses, 21,760 candidate slots, 66,178 trainable parameters and 2,944
persistent state scalars per sample. Reversing the fine and global feature
ranges supplied the same-resource tree-placement control.

The operating point used signal contrast $\Delta=0.80$, matched per-factor
train/test log-gain standard deviation $0.25$, child conductance 16 and
mechanism-neutral initialization. It was selected after the initial
severe-shift setting failed at every depth and two-seed exploratory sweeps of
gain, coupling and signal strength identified an accessible, non-ceiling
regime (Supplementary Fig.~S15). No exploratory seed entered inference.

Fresh confirmatory seeds 10200--10209 crossed aligned, zero-alignment,
trial-shuffled and reversed-placement shunting BP; aligned raw-additive BP; and
aligned or reversed-placement shunting LocalCA with shared-soma or exact-path
transport, for 270 fits. Adam ran for at most 180 epochs with
validation-based early stopping. The learning rate was 0.003 for BP and 0.0008
for LocalCA, and all comparisons were paired by seed.

The prespecified primary effect was aligned D3-minus-D1 accuracy. Topology
specificity required subtracting the corresponding zero-alignment,
shuffled-sensor or rewired-tree depth effect from the aligned depth effect. A
positive LocalCA claim separately required both a positive depth effect and a
positive aligned-minus-rewired interaction. Intervals used 50,000
seed-paired bootstrap draws, and exact two-sided sign-flip tests enumerated all
$2^{10}$ paired sign assignments.

Quality-control criteria required all 270 results to be finite, no LocalCA
fallback message, exact resource equality, at least nine of ten aligned BP seeds above
0.55 at every depth, and condition means below 0.98. A dedicated checkpoint
replay measured branch voltage, total conductance, input resistance,
activation derivative, path-gain dispersion and local-versus-autograd
conductance-gradient cosine on a fixed held-out batch.

\subsection*{Fixed-depth task-family and alignment factorial}

To test task specificity without adding physical depth, we fixed every network
at D3 $[2,1,2]$ and crossed three data-generating families, sensor alignment
$\alpha\in\{0,0.5,1\}$, serial versus literal grouped-point composition, and
BP versus path-transport LocalCA. The nested-factor positive control retained
the calibrated distal signal and three multiplicative gain factors. If
$z_{\ell}$ is the true zero-mean Gaussian log-gain factor at level $\ell$, the
signal-bearing excitatory coordinate was proportional to
\begin{equation*}
 (b_E+y\delta)\exp\!\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{3}
 \sigma_{\ell}z_{\ell}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{3}\sigma_{\ell}^{2}\right),
\end{equation*}
where $b_E$ is the excitatory baseline, $y\in\{0,1\}$ is the class label,
$\delta$ is the class-signal contrast and $\sigma_{\ell}$ is the log-gain
standard deviation at level $\ell$. All three levels used
$\sigma_{\ell}=0.25$. Subtracting half the summed variance keeps the expected
multiplicative gain equal to one.

Inhibitory sensor block $\ell$ measured
\begin{equation*}
 s_{\ell}(\alpha)=\alpha z_{\ell}
 +\sqrt{1-\alpha^2}\,\xi_{\ell},
\end{equation*}
where $\xi_{\ell}$ is an independent standard-normal nuisance factor. Here,
$\alpha\in[0,1]$ controls task--sensor alignment: $\alpha=0$ makes the observed
sensor independent of the latent gain, whereas $\alpha=1$ reveals it exactly.
Changing $\alpha$ therefore changes trial-wise task--sensor correspondence
while preserving each sensor's marginal log-gain variance. The flat-factor
control retained the factor product and level-wise marginal variances but
replaced nested cross-feature covariance with eight unrelated groups per
level. The local-ratio control instead multiplied excitation and inhibition at
each level by correlated cumulative hierarchical fields, favoring a local
divisive readout rather than serial cancellation of separate factors.

All task families used 10,000 balanced examples, 64 excitatory and 64
inhibitory features, a distal-only excitatory class signal, signal contrast
0.80, gain SD 0.25, input-noise SD 0.08, and the previously selected optimizer,
stopping and conductance settings. Serial and grouped-point models had
identical branch modules, contacts, candidate slots, trainable parameters and
input tensors; only child-to-parent composition differed. Ten fresh paired
seeds 10500--10509 produced 360 fits.

Frozen estimands were the within-seed serial-minus-grouped contrast, its
$\alpha=1$ minus $0$ interaction, the nested-family interaction minus each
mismatch-family interaction, and the corresponding LocalCA-minus-BP credit
interaction. Directional claims required a 95\% paired-seed bootstrap interval
excluding zero and at least eight of ten seed differences in the predicted
direction. The complete family scan was reported regardless of direction.
Configurations wrote only local artifacts on the
\texttt{kempner\_project\_b} filesystem, and external experiment tracking was
disabled in both configuration and runtime.

\subsection*{State-matched positive-conductance reliability experiment}

The locked mechanism experiment used eight branches, four nonnegative rate
inputs per branch, 512 training examples, 2,048 test examples and one learned
positive excitatory conductance per input. Branch voltage was
$V_b=g_b^E/(g_b^L+g_b^E)$. A nonnegative shunt $\kappa_b$ with reversal zero
was paired with the oracle current $\kappa_bV_b$, making the post-intervention
voltage exactly
$(g_b^E+\kappa_bV_b)/(g_b^L+g_b^E+\kappa_b)=V_b$. At the same time, local
eligibility was attenuated by
$(g_b^L+g_b^E)/(g_b^L+g_b^E+\kappa_b)$. This state clamp therefore separated
physical input-resistance attenuation from suppression of the forward
feature.

Initial full-data gradients defined branch signal energy $S_b$. Independent
coefficient noise was scaled so that
$N_b=S_b\exp(-2hx_b)$ for eight equally spaced $x_b\in[-1,1]$ and
$h\in\{0,0.5,1,1.5,2\}$. With the actual step $\eta=c/L$ and $c=0.5$, shunts
were fixed from the initial oracle attenuation
$\min\{1,S_b/[c(S_b+N_b)]\}$ or from the step-consistent best global,
shuffled or reversed control. The shunts were not learned. Additional controls
were clean exact backpropagation, noisy credit without a shunt, a state-matched
additive current, an explicit point gate receiving the same factors, and
aligned attenuation with clean credit.

Forty common-noise full-data updates used one half of the numerically estimated
initial smoothness limit. This correction was frozen after the preceding pilot
was found to pair that half step with the $c=1$ gain. Two new artifact-only
canary seeds preceded 50 fresh paired confirmatory seeds 8100--8149. Frozen
gates required nonnegative inputs and shunts, voltage matching and
independently computed point/additive equivalence within $10^{-12}$, and
relative finite-difference errors below $10^{-5}$ for both the unshunted
objective and the shunted frozen-clamp surrogate. The observed maxima were
$2.22\times10^{-16}$ for state mismatch,
$4.00\times10^{-15}$ and $1.31\times10^{-14}$ for the two equivalence gates,
and $3.60\times10^{-8}$ for the physical finite-difference error. Intervals
used 20,000 resamples of paired seeds, and paired tests were two-sided
Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. The configuration, runner hashes, all 2,250
seed--condition rows and prespecified contract are supplied with the Source
Data.

The adaptive extension retained the same task, model and state clamp but
replaced oracle moments with running estimates from paired, conditionally
independent noisy observations of the local branch gradient. The design was
frozen before any canary or confirmatory outcome was inspected. Fifty fresh
paired seeds completed all 1,050 planned outcomes, and an independently

\subsection*{Same-span noisy coefficient-learning experiment}

(Supplementary Fig.~S7). Because a
spatial effect shared by backpropagation reflects forward connectivity, this
cohort was not used to support topology-specific credit transport.

The fixed-budget depth cohort used the synthetic task and contained 320 runs.
The retained 160 additive runs crossed four depths, three local feedback
fields and matched backpropagation at ten paired seeds while fixing 16
terminal branches and 960--968 active contacts per soma. The cohort therefore
matches terminal branches and input contacts, but not compartment count or
trainable-parameter count.

For the prospective fixed-checkpoint diagnostic, matched-width/scalar-fallback,
neuron-specific and exact teaching fields were evaluated at 120 input-valid backpropagation
checkpoints on one frozen test batch of 128 examples. Only nonsomatic
dendritic stages and their trainable branch parameters entered the field and
gradient vectors. Scaled capture was one minus the squared residual after the
candidate vector was optimally rescaled to the exact vector (Supplementary
Fig.~S9).

For the learning endpoint, each candidate gradient was normalized to the
exact-gradient norm and applied without additional training. The step size was
$r\lVert\theta\rVert/\lVert\nabla\mathcal L\rVert$ for
$r\in\{10^{-6},10^{-5},10^{-4},10^{-3}\}$. The primary plotted operating
point was $10^{-5}$, and all four values are supplied in the Source Data.
Relative progress was the candidate loss decrease divided by the loss decrease
from the norm-matched exact update. The association interval resampled
checkpoints while preserving the paired scalar and neuron-specific fields.

\subsection*{MICrONS morphology processing}

Cells were drawn from the functionally co-registered MICrONS cohort and
resolved from stable nucleus identifiers to roots in materialization 1822 of
\texttt{minnie65\_public}. Skeletons were obtained through the MICrONS skeleton
service, and incoming synapses came from \texttt{synapses\_pni\_2}. The
original eight cells formed a convenience pilot selected under an earlier
analysis contract rather than a random or representative sample. Anatomical
claims from this cohort are therefore explicitly single-animal and
descriptive.

Degree-two dendritic chains were collapsed into branch-to-branch segments.
Synapses were assigned to the nearest dendritic cable node and retained when
the distance was at most $5\,\mu$m. Presynaptic coarse type provided E/I
identity where available. Otherwise, a high-confidence target-structure
prediction supplied a secondary proxy from the in-development
\texttt{synapse\_target\_predictions\_ssa\_v2} table. This table does not yet
have a stable public release identifier and is not required for the
direct-type-only or disjoint-v661 analyses. The direct-type-only analysis
removed all proxy-classified contacts.

Raw axial coupling was proportional to radius squared divided by cable length.
It was normalized by the nonzero median within each cell and clipped to
0.05--20. Raw leak was proportional to radius multiplied by the larger of cable
length and radius. It was likewise normalized by the nonzero cell median and
clipped to 0.1--10. Excitatory and inhibitory synaptic areas were normalized
by the median nonzero total synaptic area within each cell. These quantities
serve as sensitivity weights rather than fitted physical conductances. The
soma was the root of every compressed tree. Ancestry matrices were constructed
by explicit parent traversal and checked for acyclicity and a unique path to
the soma.

For the disjoint sensitivity cohort, a 55-cell eligibility universe was frozen
before outcomes were inspected. All candidates were mapped to the public
MICrONS v661 static release, and the original eight cells were excluded by
stable nucleus identifier. The remaining 47 cells all passed the structural
criteria. Only direct presynaptic E/I annotations were used. Before spatial
mapping, direct labels were available for 11,024 of 234,301 incoming synapses
(4.71\%); 10,516 contacts mapped to retained cables, with a median of 212
directly typed synapses per cell. The cohort was not a population-random
sample and contained 34 L2 IT, 2 L3 IT, 10 L4 IT and 1 L5 ET cell. The same
structural-capacity procedure was applied to both cohorts, but their results
were not pooled.

For the independent-animal replication, we used the public MICrONS phase-1
layer-2/3 ``Pinky'' v185 release, a
$250\times140\times90\,\mu$m visual-cortex volume from a P36 male mouse that
is biologically distinct from \texttt{minnie65}
\citep{turner2022neocortex,microns_pinky_v185}. Before mesh topology or
selected-cell synapse counts were opened, the 362 rows labeled excitatory in
the archived soma-valence table were ordered along the second volume
coordinate and divided into 12 equal-count strata. Within each stratum, we
selected the cell nearest the global median of the first and third coordinates,
using root ID to break ties. This outcome-independent rule spans the volume
while reducing edge truncation; it is not a random population sample.

For each selected HDF5 mesh, we added the published
\texttt{link\_edges} and retained the largest connected component. This step
removed detached segmentation fragments before the soma root was selected.
The retained mesh was skeletonized with the meshparty TEASAR implementation
using a $7.5\,\mu$m soma-collapse radius and a $12\,\mu$m invalidation
distance. Cable radius was estimated as half the opposite-surface ray distance
at skeleton mesh anchors using an Embree ray index.

Incoming synapses came from the archived dense v185 table. E/I identity was
assigned only when the presynaptic root had an excitatory or inhibitory label
in the same soma-valence table. We then applied the same $5\,\mu$m mapping
gate, branch-chain compression, electrical normalization and route
dictionaries used in the v661 analysis, without retuning. The frozen QC gate
required at least 20 mapped typed inputs, including at least three inhibitory
inputs. All selected cells and exclusion reasons remain in the manifest. The
primary endpoint was sign agreement with \texttt{minnie65} for K=4
ancestry-route capture relative to random paths, depth bins and shuffled
ancestry. The mouse, not the 12 nested cells, is the biological replication
unit; cell-bootstrap intervals describe only within-volume stability.

\subsection*{MICrONS inhibitory-census analysis}

Before evaluating any endpoint, the inhibitory analysis joined the 47-cell
v661 cohort to the published v795 census using stable nucleus identifiers. All
20 overlapping targets were retained. We used the published cell-type table,
inhibitory-synapse table, complete input-synapse table and target SWCs
\citep{schneidermizell2025inhibitory}. Raw all-input coordinates were mapped
to the published slanted micrometre frame with an affine transformation fitted
from 80,114 synapse locations present in both coordinate systems. The maximum
transformation residual was $1.64\times10^{-12}\,\mu$m. Input and known
inhibitory locations were mapped to the nearest target skeleton node and
retained within $5\,\mu$m. Inputs without a known presynaptic inhibitory cell
are described as generic rather than excitatory because the census does not
label every incoming contact.

For each multisynaptic inhibitory connection, the largest contact was retained
within every published axonal clump. Each retained contact was matched to 64
generic input segments in the same compartment with the nearest log path
distance to the soma. Five hundred independent matched assignments estimated
the null distribution within each connection. Pairwise outcomes were
cable-tree distance, normalized shared path to the lowest common ancestor, and
cosine overlap between the two input-size-weighted binary descendant domains.
Outcomes were first averaged over inhibitory connections within each
postsynaptic target. The target, rather than an individual contact or
connection, was the inferential unit.

Two sensitivity analyses addressed dependence and spatial matching. First,
connection-level contrasts were averaged within each of 92 presynaptic axons
before inference. Second, candidate locations were ranked jointly by log path
distance and three-dimensional Euclidean proximity to the observed contact.
The ranking used the sum of the two within-pool percentile ranks, after which
the same 500 matched assignments were repeated. These analyses test whether a
target-level contrast persists after clustering repeated connections from the
same axon and after incorporating local tissue proximity into the null.

For the route-capacity analysis, rows represented segments with mapped input
and columns represented segments bearing at least one known inhibitory
contact. The binary ancestry matrix was weighted by the square root of mapped
input size across rows and known inhibitory size across columns. Actual sites
were ranked by column norm. At each budget, selected columns were used to
reconstruct the full weighted actual-route matrix. Controls comprised 200
random subsets of actual sites, depth bins, depth-preserving within-column
ancestry shuffles and an unconstrained SVD oracle.

Leverage selection is a capacity diagnostic rather than a biological learning
rule. Because both the selected columns and the reconstruction target come
from the same actual-route matrix, this endpoint measures internal dictionary
compressibility, not generalization to an independently generated credit
field.

\subsection*{Credit dictionaries and capture}

The weighted direct route matrix was $\Gamma_{ik}=A_{ik}\beta_k$. It is a
normalized route-sensitivity proxy rather than the exact derivative of the
reciprocal passive-cable solve. Perturbation fields were generated by
multiplying independent route amplitudes by $\Gamma$. At each channel budget,
nested-subtree routes were ranked by $\beta_k$ multiplied by the fraction of total
mapped excitatory synaptic area among their descendants. Controls were an
unconstrained principal-component basis, random nonempty real paths, cable-depth
bins and column-wise ancestry shuffles. Channel count and the number of
evaluated examples were matched. Feedback wiring density was defined as the
fraction of nonzero entries in the site-by-channel dictionary.

For each dictionary, weighted least squares provided the optimal projection
after both the field and dictionary were left-multiplied by $W^{1/2}$, where
$W$ contains mapped excitatory synaptic area. Numerical sensitivity was assessed with
twenty independent Monte Carlo streams, each containing 384 perturbation fields
and 64 random or shuffled dictionaries per cell and channel count. Streams
were not treated as biological replicates. Primary comparisons were averaged
within each cell before paired testing.

For the independent-generator control, the reconstruction target was not
sampled from $\Gamma$. We added a small focal shunt at every
inhibitory-bearing segment of the reciprocal passive-cable system, restored
baseline somatic voltage with an independently solved state-matching
compensatory current, and finite-differenced the log magnitude of the exact
excitatory-conductance gradient at every excitatory-bearing segment. The
derivative dose was $10^{-3}$ times the focal segment's
membrane-plus-synaptic conductance. This procedure produced a weighted response
operator $H$ whose columns were exact focal responses.

For a dictionary $\Phi$, expected field-energy capture was calculated without
Monte Carlo sampling as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fieldcapture}
\frac{\lVert P_{W^{1/2}\Phi}W^{1/2}H\rVert_F^2}
{\lVert W^{1/2}H\rVert_F^2},
\end{equation}
where $P$ denotes the orthogonal projector onto the stated column space. In addition to the primary
controls, 200 surrogate trees per cell reassigned child subtrees among parent
slots at the preceding depth. This preserved all node and edge attributes,
each node's topological depth, and each parent's out-degree while changing
ancestral membership. A separate row shuffle preserved selected route values
and sparsity but broke their assignment to excitatory segments.

The coarse-to-fine analysis of the non-scalar ancestry-route field
(Supplementary Fig.~S25) used a deterministic weighted tree-Haar construction
on each unbalanced reconstructed arbor. Supplementary Section~S7 defines the
construction, column-permuted ancestry control and cohort statistics.

\subsection*{Focal conductance perturbations}

Before the focal analysis, we fixed the site-selection rule, primary dose,
comparison groups and localization endpoint. A depth-stratified rule selected
sites with at least three excitatory descendants and three comparison sites,
with at most 16 sites per cell sampled across topological-depth quartiles.
Primary excitatory and inhibitory synaptic scales were both 0.35, with
normalized reversal potentials $E_E=1$ and $E_I=-0.2$. Conductance doses were
0.25, 0.5, 1 and 2 times the local membrane-plus-synaptic conductance. The
additive control injected the first-order current
$\kappa_k(E_I-V_k)$ at the same node without changing $G$. After either
intervention, an independently solved compensatory current restored baseline
somatic voltage. This intervention is not the derivative of a continuously
adjusted voltage-clamp controller.

The objective was squared somatic error, with target voltage set one normalized
unit below baseline. Excitatory-conductance gradients were obtained by an
adjoint solve, with the solved compensatory current treated as fixed during
the post-intervention derivative, and were checked by finite differences. Let
$d_i=E_E-V_i$ and $d'_i=E_E-V'_i$. The post-shunt gradient $q'_id'_i$ was
decomposed algebraically into an adjoint-only substitution $q'_id_i$ and a
driving-force-only substitution $q_id'_i$, alongside the full-shunt and
matched-additive gradients.

Two-factor Shapley values allocated the nonlinear
full-shunt-minus-additive localization payoff between adjoint and driving-force
changes and reconciled exactly to the observed contrast at each site. Because
the matched-additive reference is not a literal no-factor physical
intervention, we also calculated an unperturbed-reference allocation as a
sensitivity analysis. These substitutions are algebraic controls rather than
independent biological interventions.

Synapses were classified as descendants, ancestors, sister-subtree sites or
unrelated sites. Within each template, unrelated synapses were matched to
descendant cable depth. For baseline gradient $g_i$ and perturbed gradient
$g_i'$, we defined
$m_i=\left|\log[(|g_i'|+\varepsilon)/(|g_i|+\varepsilon)]\right|$, where
$\varepsilon=10^{-15}\max(1,\max_i|g_i|)$. The frozen site-level localization
index was the median $m_i$ among descendants minus the median among
depth-matched unrelated synapses. Site effects were averaged within cells, and
the primary paired contrast used the eight cell means. A topology control
sampled 200 relation templates from other focal sites, stratified by focal-site
topological-depth quartile when another site was available; otherwise, it used
another site in the same cell. Supplementary Section~S6 specifies axial and
leak normalization and clipping.

We also calibrated the passive cable in physical units. Nonroot segments were
modeled as cylinders with axial conductance $\pi r^2/(R_a\ell)$ and leak
conductance $2\pi r\ell/R_m$, while the root used spherical membrane area
$4\pi r^2$. Conductances were expressed in nS. Because synaptic areas are not
conductance measurements, excitatory and inhibitory synaptic conductances
retained their prespecified relative scale and were referenced to the
cell-median physical leak conductance.

We evaluated axial resistivities
\[
R_a\in\{100,150,250\}\,\Omega\,\mathrm{cm}
\]
at $R_m=15{,}000\,\Omega\,\mathrm{cm}^2$. In a separate sweep with
$R_a=150\,\Omega\,\mathrm{cm}$, membrane resistivity took the values
\[
R_m\in\{300,1000,3000,5000,15{,}000,30{,}000\}\,
\Omega\,\mathrm{cm}^2.
\]
The same sites, state-matching current, adjoint
gradient, unit relative dose and additive comparison were retained. Two
zero-length nonroot segments in the v661 source skeletons used their radius as
the cable length. This fallback and all electrotonic ratios are recorded in
the Source Data.

The frozen passive focal-sensitivity matrix used the original eight cells at
$R_a=150\,\Omega\,\mathrm{cm}$. We crossed membrane resistivities of 300,
1000 and $15{,}000\,\Omega\,\mathrm{cm}^2$ with distributed leak-like
background conductance equal to 0, 1 or 4 times each segment's baseline leak.
At every selected site, three dose schemes were evaluated: 0.25, 1 and 2 times
local membrane-plus-synaptic conductance; fixed doses of 0.05, 0.5 and 5 nS;
and 0.25, 1 and 4 times local input conductance
$[(G^{-1})_{kk}]^{-1}$. Rank-one voltage and adjoint updates were calculated
exactly with Sherman--Morrison, followed by the same state-matching somatic
current.

In addition to absolute localization, frozen outputs included signed
log-magnitude change, attenuated and enhanced fractions, sign-flip fraction,
descendant gradient-energy ratio, local input resistance and $S_k$ from
Eq.~\ref{eq:selectivitycriterion}. Site values were averaged within cells
before eight-cell intervals and shunt-minus-additive contrasts were computed.
A preliminary numerical check required positive-definite matrices and a
somatic residual below $10^{-10}$ before the full matrix was run with unchanged
code and configuration.

For the active steady-state sensitivity, voltage-dependent Na, K, Ca, HCN and
NMDA currents were added to the physical passive system at
$R_a=150\,\Omega\,$cm, $R_m=1{,}000\,\Omega\,$cm$^2$ and one
baseline-leak unit of distributed background conductance. Channel activation
used sigmoidal steady-state gates with channel-specific half-activation,
slope, power and reversal values fixed in the machine-readable protocol.
Global and segment-level maximal-conductance multipliers were independent
lognormal draws. Newton iterations with a residual-reducing line search solved
the full nonlinear equilibrium.

Draws were accepted only when voltage remained within the prespecified range,
the residual was below $10^{-10}$ and the symmetrized local Jacobian was
positive definite above $10^{-8}$. With purely local voltage-dependent gates,
off-diagonal Jacobian entries are the reciprocal axial couplings. The
steady-state Jacobian is therefore symmetric, so the symmetrized gate and the
plain inverse used below are exact rather than approximate.

For every accepted equilibrium, the adjoint used the exact inverse of the
local Jacobian. Focal rank-one shunts and first-order-current-matched additive
inputs were evaluated at 0.25, 1 and 4 times local input conductance, followed
by the same state-matching compensatory current at the soma. We required 64
accepted draws in every cell, allowing up to 256 attempts; all 512 first
attempts were accepted. The 64 channel draws and up to 16 focal sites were
averaged within each reconstructed cell before bootstrap intervals and paired
tests. The active ensemble is a steady-state local-linearization sensitivity
analysis, not a simulation of channel kinetics or spikes.

The focal analysis was repeated after all proxy-classified contacts were
removed. In the disjoint v661 sensitivity cohort, 45 of 47 cells provided 235
eligible focal sites for the primary shunt--additive comparison. Forty cells
with at least two eligible sites provided the reassigned-relation control.
Cells, not focal sites, remained the inferential units.

\subsection*{Measured-response analyses}

Functionally imaged presynaptic partners were linked to anatomical contacts
through the MICrONS/CAVE and DANDI mappings. The original frozen one-scan
cohort contained 69 partners across seven targets. The scan-complete analysis
deterministically retained every automatic-conservative
target--session--scan cohort with at least five unique connected imaged
presynaptic roots, yielding 13 scan observations nested within the same seven
targets. Each scan contained 464 trials, 280 stimulus identities and 136
repeated identities.

Multiple contacts from one presynaptic partner were pooled, and its analysis
site was the segment with the greatest summed contact area. Functional
similarity was the Pearson correlation of condition-mean responses over
repeated identities. For each target, the partial-rank analysis rank
transformed functional similarity and shared ancestry, residualized each
against log Euclidean separation and path-depth difference, and correlated the
two residuals. Scan-level statistics were averaged within targets before the
target bootstrap and signed-rank test. The target neuron, not an individual
scan or synaptic pair, was the inferential unit.

For task-derived credit, a small branch model predicted each postsynaptic
response from the responses of its connected presynaptic partners. Complete
visual-stimulus identities were assigned to held-out splits. Exact branch
errors were calculated on training trials, and dictionary projectors were fit
without using held-out identities. Matched models were then trained from the
same initialization with exact or projected branch errors. Ten fixed
split/initialization replicates were averaged within each of seven target
cells.

Because the projection coefficients used exact errors, these analyses estimate
the capacity of a feedback family rather than a biological encoder. Only
input-weight errors were projected; branch-readout weights and bias received
exact gradients. Normalized MSE was held-out MSE divided by the held-out error
of the training-mean predictor. Supplementary Section~S9 provides complete
model and optimizer specifications.

For the segment-resolved analysis, each complete compressed reconstruction
was instantiated as a reciprocal passive conductance system. A trainable
nonnegative excitatory conductance was placed at the dominant mapped contact
of every imaged partner. Exact gradients of somatic squared prediction error
used the trial-specific inverse conductance operator and factorized into
$x_i(E_E-V_i)$ multiplied by the segment adjoint.

Restricted methods first projected the baseline soma-to-site transfer vector
into one of three four-channel dictionaries: conductance-weighted ancestry, a
column-value-preserving site shuffle, or an equal-rank random set of nonempty
anatomical routes. This vector was calibrated once without target responses
and then frozen during learning. Each restricted update used only scalar
output error, the fixed site vector and local eligibility. All methods used
exact readout and bias gradients.

Ten seed-fixed splits held out complete stimulus identities within every scan.
Replicates were averaged within scans, and all eligible scans were then
averaged within targets before seven-cell inference. Finite differences
audited one exact conductance coordinate per run, and input and runner hashes
were archived. Any absent route, rank mismatch, nonfinite value or incomplete
scan would have caused exclusion; none occurred.

The capture--learning association excluded exact and dense oracles. Both
variables were centered within targets before Spearman's $\rho$ was computed.
Its interval used 20,000 target-level bootstrap draws. The two-sided null used
20,000 independent reassignments of learning outcomes among method labels
within each target.

\subsection*{Alignment-controlled optimization}

For each reconstructed cell, we rebuilt the conductance-weighted ancestry
kernel and, before generating gradient fields, selected eight routes according
to inhibitory fraction multiplied by descendant excitatory synaptic-area
fraction. Segment coordinates were weighted by the square root of summed
mapped excitatory synaptic area. If $Q$ is an orthonormal basis for the
selected routes, each unit-energy exact gradient was constructed as
\begin{equation}
\bm g(a)=\sqrt{a}\,\bm u_{\parallel}+\sqrt{1-a}\,\bm u_{\perp},
\end{equation}
where $\bm u_{\parallel}\in\operatorname{span}(Q)$,
$\bm u_{\perp}\perp\operatorname{span}(Q)$ and
$a\in\{0,0.2,0.4,0.6,0.8,1\}$. Directions were paired across alignment
levels. Controls were eight random real paths, eight cable-depth bins and
row-permuted nested-subtree routes. The permutation preserved column values and
nonzero count while breaking ancestry.

Each cell contributed 40 nested Monte Carlo streams with 256 gradients per
stream. The objective was a positive diagonal quadratic with an
anatomy-independent curvature spectrum from 0.5 to 1.5, permuted within each
stream and held fixed across methods and alignment levels. One-step updates
had a norm equal to 0.1 times the exact-gradient norm. Iterative projected
descent used learning rate 0.25 for 20 steps. Both outcomes were normalized by
the corresponding full-gradient progress. Streams and gradient fields assessed
numerical sensitivity, while cell-level means served as the inferential
units.

\subsection*{External animal-data reanalysis}

Source data from Francioni et al. were obtained from the public Nature record
associated with DOI 10.1038/s41586-026-10190-7. The archived workbook was
verified by SHA-256 before analysis. Extended Data sheet 13E provided one P+
and one P- contrast per animal, defined in the source study as the change in
soma--dendrite residual between error-reduction and error-increase periods. We
retained the source signs. Signed separation was calculated as P+ minus P- and
checked against the value reported in the workbook.

The common basis was $(1,1)/\sqrt{2}$ and the signed basis was
$(1,-1)/\sqrt{2}$; squared projection norms were divided by total
two-coordinate energy. Sheet 5E supplied neuron-level distributions for
visualization only. Directional animal-level hypotheses followed the known
causal mapping. We report exact random-sign and Wilcoxon tests together with
animal-bootstrap intervals based on 50,000 draws.

\subsection*{Static task-interference calculation}

The task-interference identity follows from an exact quadratic expansion around
a Task-A optimum, where the linear term is zero. In the equal-route
illustration, each task occupied 1,000 coordinates, route overlap ranged from
zero to one, and the Task-B target opposed Task A on shared coordinates.
Off-route leakage supplied an update of relative magnitude $\lambda$ on
Task-A-only coordinates. We evaluated a $101\times101$ grid over overlap and
leakage at step size 0.2 and compared explicit vector updates with the closed
form at every grid point. This analysis contains no temporal or spiking neural
dynamics. The published motor-learning study is used only for qualitative
cross-study interpretation.

\subsection*{Alignment-by-bandwidth synthesis plane}

The synthesis panel in Fig.~\ref{fig:creditphase}g is a deterministic
secondary display assembled from frozen outputs; it does not rerun any
experiment. Its horizontal coordinate is task--anatomy alignment, defined as
the fraction of task-gradient or field energy in the anatomical route span.
Its vertical coordinate is relative feedback bandwidth
$K/r_{\mathrm{eff}}$ on a logarithmic scale, where effective task-credit rank
is
$r_{\mathrm{eff}}=(\sum_i\lambda_i)^2/\sum_i\lambda_i^2$ for the relevant
credit spectrum. Both coordinates are estimated separately for each
experimental family.

For the 2,700-fit factorial, alignment is the mean initial exact-gradient
capture of the correct-ancestry span in the dendritic-tree implementation, and
$r_{\mathrm{eff}}=7.99$ comes from the frozen task spectra. For the spectral
sweep, alignment is the imposed covariance-mixture coefficient $\rho$, and
$r_{\mathrm{eff}}(\rho)$ is estimated within seeds from dense-PCA cumulative
capture. For the alignment-controlled MICrONS experiment, alignment is the
imposed aligned-energy fraction $a$. Its bandwidth coordinate is derived
analytically from the documented field generator as the geometric mean across
cells of $a^2+8(1-a)^2/(n-8)$, where $n$ is the cell's weighted coordinate
count; it is not read from a results table. For the coarse measured-response
null, alignment is mean held-out field capture by nested-subtree routes, and
$r_{\mathrm{eff}}=1.07$ is estimated from dense-oracle capture at one through
eight channels. For the two-stream credit-reversal task, alignment is the
initial scaled capture of correct two-subtree routing, and
$r_{\mathrm{eff}}\approx2.0$ follows from the algebraic rank-two expression
$1/[c^2+(1-c)^2]$, with $c=0.510$ denoting shared-coordinate capture.

The full-tree measured-response null is not plotted because its task rank was
not estimated comparably. Marker fill records each family's frozen win, tie or
loss for the ancestry-versus-control contrast, and region tints are derived
from theory rather than fit to the results. Every coordinate, definition and
source is listed in
\texttt{source\_data/credit\_phase\_plane/points.csv}.

\subsection*{Interior-optimum and wiring-normalized reanalyses}

Two deterministic secondary analyses of frozen outputs support
Supplementary Fig.~S16 and Fig.~\ref{fig:topology}e,f. Neither analysis alters
or reruns a training outcome. For the interior-optimum analysis, per-seed
initialization utility from Eq.~\ref{eq:phaseutility} was taken from the

depth-routing tables. The predicted optimum was the maximum utility over routed
depth, or the maximum ancestry-minus-best-control utility contrast over

ancestry-minus-best-control accuracy contrast
(0.0127; 15 of 20 seeds) from the frozen seed outcomes.

For the wiring-normalized analysis, capture per unit wiring was defined as
weighted field-energy capture divided by feedback wiring density. It was
calculated within cells from frozen routing-capacity curves after Monte Carlo
streams were averaged within each cell. The published eight-channel capture
and wiring anchors were reproduced before any ratio was formed. Intervals are
95\% seed- or cell-bootstrap intervals based on 20,000 resamples. The
laminar-capture proposition and marginal utility condition
(Supplementary Section~S4) are verified numerically in the released test
suite.

\subsection*{Statistics and reproducibility}

Unless a directional hypothesis had been frozen in advance, all tests were
two-sided. Central comparisons are reported with effect sizes and 95\%
intervals. Reconstructed-cell intervals resampled cells first and, when
appropriate, one Monte Carlo stream within each cell. Textual
focal-attribution intervals used a hierarchical bootstrap over cells and
sites. By contrast, the dose-response display in
Fig.~\ref{fig:focal}c shows cell-bootstrap intervals after site values were
averaged within each cell. Measured-response intervals resampled target cells,
whereas artificial-network means and standard deviations used independent
initialization seeds. Exact Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used for small
paired cell cohorts. No site, trial, epoch, or Monte Carlo stream was treated
as an independent biological replicate. Null results are described as a lack
of reliable evidence rather than as proof of equality.

Bootstrap intervals used 20,000 draws unless stated otherwise in the Methods.
The Fashion-MNIST ladder, physical-depth, H4, task-family, and external-animal
analyses used 50,000 paired draws. Benjamini--Hochberg correction was applied
within each frozen primary family that prespecified it: the four-budget subtree
scan, the six bandwidth-matched routing contrasts, and the frozen H4 primary
family. Other parameter sweeps and sensitivity analyses are labeled secondary
and are not treated as independent confirmation.

Seed counts reflected the evidential role of each cohort rather than a
single pooled power calculation. Three to five seeds were reserved for
numerical or mechanism diagnostics, ten for fresh paired confirmatory
replications with interval and sign-agreement gates, 15--20 for inherited
trained factorials, and 50 for signal--noise boundary and crossover distributions.
Because effect sizes were unknown before the new simulation families were
run, we did not use a prospective parametric power calculation. Positive
confirmatory claims required both a paired 95\% interval excluding zero and
the prespecified fraction of concordant seed signs. Diagnostic cohorts were
not promoted to inferential evidence. A machine-readable table in Source Data
maps every headline endpoint to its independent unit and identifies nested or
descriptive observations.

The analysis code records configurations, seeds, software versions, source
tables, and machine-readable summaries. Numerical source data are organized by
figure and panel in the accompanying package. The test suite covers tree
construction, projection identities, and exact-gradient checks.
